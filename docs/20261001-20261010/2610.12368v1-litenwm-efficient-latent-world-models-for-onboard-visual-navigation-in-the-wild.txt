Abstract —Direct visual navigation policies generate trajecto-ries efficiently but do not explicitly evaluate their future conse-quences. Generative navigation world models provide this fore-sight through visual rollouts, which are costly when evaluating multiple candidates. We present LiteNWM, a latent navigation world model that shares visual encoding across candidates and jointly predicts their action-conditioned future representations at multiple horizons and a learned scorer uses these predictions to select trajectories. In offline evaluations on RECON, SCAND, and SACSoN, LiteNWM reduces macro-averaged trajectory error by 17.56% relative to NoMaD+NWM-XL and achieves a 128.00 ×

end-to-end speedup on an RTX 5090. The same evaluator trans-fers from NoMaD to MBRA without proposer-specific retraining, reducing MBRA’s macro-averaged trajectory error by 16.2%. In real-robot experiments in unseen indoor and outdoor envi-ronments, LiteNWM improves navigation success from 43.3% to 83.3% relative to NoMaD. These results demonstrate that LiteNWM can be deployed for future-aware planning and closed-loop navigation on a physical robot. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Goal-conditioned visual navigation enables a robot to reach a visually specified target using egocentric observations [1]. Recent generalist navigation policies, such as GNM, ViNT, and NoMaD, learn navigation from large and diverse robot datasets [2]–[4]. Diffusion-based policies can further generate multiple plausible trajectories for each observation. However, these policies select trajectories without explicitly evaluating their future consequences, which can lead to suboptimal deci-sions in challenging environments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, Navigation World Models (NWM) address this limitation through future-aware trajectory selec-tion [5]. Given multiple trajectories proposed by a navigation policy, NWM predicts future visual observations conditioned on each candidate and selects the trajectory based on its predicted outcome. In our component profile with prediction horizon H = 4 and 

K = 16 candidate trajectories, candidate-conditioned RGB rollout accounts for 97.6% of the captured NoMaD+NWM-XL computation (Fig. 3). Its end-to-end wall time is 221.97 s per decision state (Sec. IV-E), motivating a less expensive evaluation path. We present LiteNWM, a navigation trajectory evaluator de-signed around the rollout bottleneck identified above. Action-conditioned latent prediction already supports navigation with-out RGB reconstruction. LiteNWM targets candidate-wise rollout cost through shared visual encoding and joint H1– H4 prediction across candidates. A learned incumbent-relative scorer uses the predicted consequences and proposal-set con-text to guide trajectory selection. This design combines an efficient prediction path with relative outcome evaluation while keeping the navigation policies frozen. We evaluate LiteNWM on development cohorts from three navigation datasets and examine its deployment on a phys-ical robot. LiteNWM improves trajectory accuracy while achieving 128.00 × and 16.86 × end-to-end speedups over NoMaD+NWM-XL and NoMaD+NWM-S, respectively, on arXiv:2610.12368v1 [cs.RO] 8 Oct 2026 an RTX 5090 (Fig. 1(d)). The frozen evaluator also trans-fers to MBRA without proposer-specific retraining. Ablations attribute the accuracy gain to dynamic multi-horizon latent prediction. LiteNWM supports closed-loop navigation on a Unitree Go2 EDU in unseen environments. Our main con-tributions are:  

> •

We identify candidate-wise RGB rollout as the dominant evaluation cost and restructure the evaluation path around shared encoding and joint candidate-future prediction.  

> •

We combine pose-conditioned prediction with incumbent-relative scoring to compare candidate consequences using proposal-set context.  

> •

We validate accuracy and end-to-end efficiency on matched initial proposal pools, achieving a 128.00 ×

speedup over NoMaD+NWM-XL on an RTX 5090, and demonstrate cross-proposer transfer and real-robot de-ployment. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Generalist Navigation Policies and Trajectory Selection 

Learning-based visual navigation has increasingly shifted toward policies trained on heterogeneous robot experience. GNM introduced a shared goal-conditioned formulation across robot platforms [2], while ViNT scaled this direction with a Transformer trained on diverse navigation data [3]. NoMaD further combines goal masking with a diffusion decoder to represent multimodal navigation behaviors [4], [18]. Proposal generation and trajectory selection become distinct operations when multiple stochastic trajectories are exposed at inference. NavDP couples diffusion-based trajectory generation with a learned critic for selection [6], while ViLiNT ranks generated trajectories using an embodiment-conditioned path-clearance predictor [7]. LiteNWM keeps trajectory generation fixed and focuses on evaluating the resulting proposals using predicted future evidence. 

B. Generative World Models for Navigation 

Visual world models provide such evidence by predicting observations along candidate action sequences. Pathdreamer generates unobserved panoramic views for look-ahead plan-ning in indoor navigation [8]. Moreover, NWM predicts future RGB observations conditioned on candidate actions and uses the synthesized futures both for standalone planning and for ranking trajectories sampled from an external policy [5]. NWM therefore establishes that future-conditioned trajectory reranking and a modular proposer–evaluator interface are prior art. The question narrows to whether short-horizon trajectory candidates can instead be compared directly from action-conditioned future latent features rather than reconstructing an RGB future for every candidate. 

C. Latent Predictive and World–Action Models 

Latent world models provide an alternative by supporting decision making directly through learned predictive repre-sentations. Such explicit visual generation also introduces computation that is not directly required by the downstream ranking objective, which is well established by previous work: PlaNet performs online planning in learned latent dynamics [9], while TD-MPC2 optimizes trajectories using a decoder-free implicit world model [10]. More recently, DINO-WM predicts action-conditioned DI-NOv2 patch features for test-time action optimization [12], V-JEPA 2-AC enables model-predictive control through action-conditioned latent prediction [11], [19], and DINO-world ex-tends feature-space prediction to a generalist video model that can be adapted to observation–action trajectories [13]. DF 3

further demonstrates decoder-free feature forecasting, though its current formulation is observation-only and cannot model the consequences of alternative actions [14]. ReL-NWM avoids RGB reconstruction through latent-space planning [15], but retains sequential autoregressive rollouts. LiteNWM targets rollout latency through shared visual encoding and joint multi-horizon prediction, eliminating autoregressive feedback across prediction horizons. In contrast, WAM-Nav and NavWAM take a different direc-tion by coupling visual foresight with action generation inside unified world–action policies [16], [17]. LiteNWM addresses a narrower question: whether action-conditioned latent pre-diction provides sufficient predictive evidence to distinguish among trajectory proposals from a frozen navigation policy. C. Training, Trajectory Selection, and Execution 

Training and calibration. We sequentially train three-member predictor and scorer ensembles, keeping NoMaD and DI-NOv3 frozen. Predictor losses comprise latent regression, co-sine, action-sensitivity, temporal-consistency, and uncertainty terms. Scorer losses comprise heteroscedastic regression, dual-improvement/harm classification, and pairwise/listwise rank-ing. Both stages use AdamW with weight decay 10 −4. The predictor uses learning rate 6 × 10 −5, batch size 56, gradient clipping at 1.0, and 300 warm-up steps followed by cosine decay. The scorer uses learning rate 10 −4, clipping at 5.0, and early stopping on the disjoint scorer-calibration partition.

B. End-to-end Evaluation 

Table I provides the main system-level comparison. Relative to NoMaD+NWM-XL, LiteNWM reduces ATE by 25.21% on RECON, 14.98% on SCAND, and 14.71% on SACSoN. LiteNWM obtains the lowest ATE and RPE and the highest SR@1 in all three domains. Compared with NoMaD+NWM-XL, it raises SR@1 by 6.5, 3.5, and 7.0 percentage points on RECON, SCAND, and SACSoN, respectively. Figure 5 shows that LiteNWM reduces ATE by slowing error growth over the prediction horizon. Although the meth-ods have similar errors at the initial timestep, the baseline errors increase more rapidly at later timesteps across all three domains. Because ATE aggregates errors over four future waypoints, this reduced accumulation leads to lower ATE (Table I). NWM performance may also be affected by the 

H = 8 -to-H = 4 horizon and prior mismatch, the imperfect alignment between terminal LPIPS and geometric accuracy, and the official one-step CEM update. In contrast, joint H1– H4 prediction provides intermediate and terminal evidence for rejecting candidates with accumulating errors. The next section evaluates the contribution of joint H1–H4 prediction to trajectory selection. 

C. Ablation Study 

Table II compares four representations on identical can-didate pools. All variants retain the same observed visual context and candidate actions, with scorers trained separately under matched conditions. No predicted future removes the future-feature block; Static DINO repeat replaces it with repeated current DINO features; Predicted H4 only uses only terminal-prediction features; and Predicted H1–H4 uses all four predicted horizons. Predicted futures improve selection over both controls. H4 captures most of this gain; intermediate horizons provide a smaller additional improvement by supply-ing trajectory-evolution features beyond the terminal state. To test whether the scorer uses the future associated with each candidate, we freeze the H1–H4 scorer and apply two pre-specified permutations of candidate–future pairings. Complete predicted-future feature blocks are permuted while actions, context, and model weights remain fixed; derived features are recomputed. Averaged over the two permutations, macro ATE increases by 0.0294 (95% paired cluster-bootstrap CI: 

[0 .0175 , 0.0415] ). The interval uses 20,000 dataset-stratified cluster resamples, retaining grouped states and proposal seeds. As consistency checks, jointly permuting complete candidate bundles preserves selections, and the no-prediction control is also unaffected. These results support the scorer’s use of correctly paired predicted-future evidence.

We measure end-to-end inference on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 using the same hardware, cohort, and timing boundary for all methods. The timing boundary includes proposal generation, future evaluation, scoring, refinement, and final selection. F. Real-World Deployment 

We deploy LiteNWM on a Unitree Go2 EDU. The naviga-tion policies receive only monocular RGB observations and an RGB goal image. We evaluate 10 trials per method in each of the three scenes listed in Table V. Success requires entering a calibrated 1.0-m goal radius with a visible, goal-consistent target observation and yaw error within 60 ◦, before collision, corrective intervention, or abnormal termination. The valid state must persist for at least 1 s or be followed by a normal stop; merely passing the target does not count. 

Transfer to unseen layouts. LiteNWM performs closed-loop navigation in previously unseen indoor and outdoor layouts without scene-specific training or online model up-dates. Figure 7(a) additionally illustrates representative tra-jectories through furniture-constrained spaces, outdoor areas, and corridors. These examples provide qualitative evidence of deployment across changes in scene structure, illumination, and viewpoint. 

Reliable closed-loop goal reaching. LiteNWM achieves more reliable goal reaching than NoMaD across the three eval-uated settings (Table V). Figure 7(b) shows representative comparisons in the open indoor, cluttered indoor, and outdoor courtyard scenes. The larger success-rate gains in the cluttered indoor route and outdoor courtyard suggest that the benefit extends beyond the short open-indoor route. This pattern is consistent with the future-aware selection design in Sec. III. LiteNWM continuously refreshes the visual history and pre-dicts the H1–H4 latent consequences of proposed trajectories. The scorer relates these predicted futures to the visual goal and retained incumbent, providing evidence about intermediate motion and terminal goal consistency before execution. This receding-horizon procedure repeatedly incorporates the latest observation, allowing future-aware comparisons to be updated as the robot moves. 

Efficient future-aware navigation. LiteNWM reduces the computational burden of future-aware selection, as quantified by the profiling in Table III. The deployment probes reveal the practical scale of the NWM-S planning delay: even with DDIM reduced to 30 steps, the two runs average 234.0 and 236.5 s per planning decision, over 13 and 9 decisions, re-spectively. These exploratory runs characterize computational cost and are excluded from aggregate navigation-outcome comparisons. The lower evaluation cost follows from the com-putational design in Sec. III: the history and goal are encoded once and shared across candidates, while candidate futures are evaluated jointly in latent space without RGB decoding. This preserves candidate-conditioned look-ahead while avoiding the expensive image-generation stage at each replanning step.

V. C ONCLUSION 

Combining action-conditioned multi-horizon latent predic-tion with incumbent-relative scoring, LiteNWM selects tra-jectories from a frozen policy without RGB reconstruction. On matched initial NoMaD proposal pools across RECON, SCAND, and SACSoN, it reduces macro ATE by 17.56% over NWM-XL. Ablations support the contribution of predicted future features beyond static visual context. Fixed-pool selection retains 94.75% of the observed CEM1 ATE reduction relative to NoMaD+NWM-XL (Table VII). Increasing CEM depth from one to three rounds lowers macro ATE by a further 0.64%, while raising wall time by approx-imately 71% in the RECON resource benchmark (Table X). CEM here refines policy proposals, whereas NWM [5] also uses it for standalone planning. Comparing optimization gains requires matched objectives and budgets; these experiments do not distinguish limited search coverage from errors in evaluating refined trajectories. The frozen proposer receives no training feedback from the evaluator, so its initial proposal distribution remains unchanged when LiteNWM identifies better trajectories. The four-step predictor evaluates local consequences; longer routes rely on repeated replanning and do not establish prediction accuracy beyond this horizon. More aggressive score optimization could expose model errors, particularly outside the proposal distri-bution. We aim to train the trajectory generator with scorer and predictor feedback. Inspired by DINO-WM [12], gradients of a smooth incumbent-relative cost could pass through the scorer, predictor, and candidate actions to a differentiable generator’s parameters. Evaluator weights, the incumbent, and comparison context would initially remain fixed, with updates constrained by kinematics and proximity to the pro-posal distribution. Evaluation must test observed trajectory outcomes as well as predicted scores. Comparisons with CEM and random expansion should match computational budgets, including backward-pass costs. Distilling refined trajectories could reduce repeated optimization during deployment. Beyond the predefined intermediate goal images used in the extended-route demonstrations, persistent memory and au-tonomous hierarchical subgoal selection could support detours and partially observed routes. Exploration and search-and-rescue additionally require choosing destinations and discover-ing targets. A higher-level planner could select subgoals using coverage, information gain, or semantic target detection, while LiteNWM evaluates local trajectories. Training would require targets for exploration progress, discovery, and traversability under mission time or energy constraints. Evaluation would measure coverage, discovery rate, and successful execution in unseen environments, sustained onboard latency, and whole-robot energy.
