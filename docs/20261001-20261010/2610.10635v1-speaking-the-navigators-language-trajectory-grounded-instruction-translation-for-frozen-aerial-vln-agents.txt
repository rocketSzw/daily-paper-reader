Abstract 

Aerial vision-and-language navigation (VLN) agents are typ-ically trained on detail-rich, trajectory-aligned commands, whereas users issue short, intent-driven instructions; on a frozen OpenFly navigator, this instruction gap drops success rate (SR) from 31 .03% to 11 .33% . To scale translator train-ing, we prompt a language model with human-written style examples to convert original commands into paired, intent-centered Weak commands, which yield 15 .27% SR. We in-troduce the Trajectory-Grounded Instruction Translator (TGIT) , a front-end that keeps the navigator frozen and trans-lates Weak inputs into agent-executable commands by learn-ing from its trajectory outcomes. The resulting Weak-trained translator raises Weak-input SR to 37 .93% and transfers zero-shot to real human instructions ( 11 .33% →32 .51% ); it also improves held-out OpenFly ( 4.95% →20 .79% ) and yields re-covery on CityNav and AirVLN. 

1 Introduction 

Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) has progressed from indoor instruction following (Anderson et al. 2018; Hao et al. 2020; Qiao et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2024) to aerial platforms, where UAV agents must act under continuous 3D motion, large viewpoint changes, and fine-grained spatial constraints (Liu et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025b; Saxena, Raghuvanshi, and Goveas 2025; Sautenkov et al. 2025). Yet aerial VLN agents are commonly trained on detail-dense, trajectory-aligned instructions produced by templates or vision–language generators (Gao et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2023b; Fan et al. 2024), whereas users tend to issue shorter, intent-driven commands. We begin with a simple controlled probe: keeping the routes, initial conditions, and UAV navigator fixed, we change only the instruction source. If the navigator understood its training and deployment interfaces equally well, its perfor-mance should remain stable. Instead, using natural instruc-tions collected from 16 annotators for 203 OpenFly episodes, SR drops from 31 .03% with dataset instructions to 11 .33% 

with Human instructions (Figure 1). This behavioral collapse reveals an instruction gap : deployment language fails to ac-tivate capabilities available through the same frozen agent’s training-language interface. Instruction Gap and Recoverable Activation

Closing this gap is hard for a frozen navigator trained on trajectory-aligned Orig commands: short Human instruc-tions must be rewritten into language that matches its learned associations, and two equally plausible expansions can still induce different actions. Agent-centric retraining methods can absorb this mismatch (Kamath et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2020), but are costly and may change perception and control, making it hard to tell whether gains come from adapting to new language or from altering the navigator itself (Gao et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2025b). Instruction-centric rewriting methods instead map weak in-puts back to an Orig sentence (Shah et al. 2023; Zhou, Hong, and Wu 2024; Zhang and Kordjamshidi 2023), yet match-ing that text does not tell whether a particular navigator will arXiv:2610.10635v1 [cs.AI] 7 Oct 2026 succeed. We therefore keep the navigator fixed and learn agent-executable translations from its trajectory outcomes, not from text similarity alone. This observation also determines which failures should supervise the translator. Training on arbitrary failures would conflate an instruction-interface mismatch with navigation skills that the backbone does not possess. We therefore focus on paired cases where the original instruction succeeds, but its weakened counterpart fails under the same route, initial condition, simulator, and policy. Orig success serves as a capability witness, while Weak failure isolates a recoverable interface intervention. Human annotation is too costly to scale for translator training, so we construct Weak instead: using a few Human-written instructions as style examples, an LM converts each Orig command into a shorter, intent-centered counterpart. Building on these choices, we introduce TGIT 

(Trajectory-Grounded Instruction Translator). It freezes the UAV policy and adapts only a LoRA VLM front-end that translates Weak inputs into agent-executable commands. Starting from Orig instructions in the target agent’s training dataset and their LM-generated Weak counterparts, TGIT selects hard cases, proposes multiple candidates, executes them with the frozen navigator, and converts weighted tra-jectory outcomes into preferences for multi-round DPO with pool resampling. The resulting translator is then evaluated on Weak and transferred zero-shot to Human, testing whether execution-grounded preferences activate the agent without imitating a single Orig sentence. On the 203-episode OpenFly evaluation, TGIT raises SR from 15 .27% to 37 .93% (2.48 ×), exceeding Orig ( 31 .03% ), ICL, and SFT; the same Weak-trained translator raises Human-input SR zero-shot from 11 .33% to 32 .51% (2.87 ×). Held-out OpenFly scenes and two additional frozen agents show the same relative activation, but their absolute success remains bounded by the navigator itself. These results sup-port a bounded claim: TGIT repairs recoverable instruction-interface failures, while strengthening the frozen navigator remains an orthogonal agent-centric problem. 

Our contributions are threefold: 

• Recoverable instruction gap. We provide Human evi-dence for the mismatch between deployment and training language, and operationally identify recoverable failures through original-success / Human-failure pairs. 

• Agent-executable instruction translation. We introduce TGIT, a plug-and-play front-end that can be applied to any frozen VLN agent using only that agent’s own train-ing instructions and their Weak counterparts: it builds trajectory-grounded preference data from hard cases and multi-round DPO to translate underspecified inputs into executable commands. 

• Controlled cross-agent validation. TGIT outperforms Weak, ICL, and SFT on OpenFly, transfers zero-shot from Weak training to Human inputs, and yields consistent relative gains across held-out environments and multiple frozen UAV agents. The appendix complements the main results with preliminary evidence, validity checks, and reproducibility details. A pre-liminary study further characterizes training-instruction style and agent sensitivity to underspecification, strengthening the instruction-gap motivation. Paired lexical and embedding analyses further show that Weak moves from Orig toward Human. Complete prompts, dataset statistics, optimization settings, translator-scale diagnostics, and same-route trajec-tory comparisons support reproduction and expose how the learned translations change agent behavior. 

2 Related Work 

Prior VLN and embodied-language research largely falls into two axes: improving the navigator, and rewriting or generat-ing language for clarity and coverage (Zhang et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2023; Ma et al. 2024). TGIT is not another point on either axis. It keeps the aerial policy frozen and learns an in-struction interface that activates competence already present in that policy. 

Agent-centric aerial VLN. VLN was established in pho-torealistic indoor environments (Anderson et al. 2018) and later extended toward continuous control, long-horizon nav-igation, and sim-to-real evaluation (Anderson et al. 2021; Song et al. 2025; Lin et al. 2025). AerialVLN introduced continuous outdoor UAV flight, while CityNav, OpenFly, and AeroVerse scale aerial environments, trajectories, and embodied controllers (Liu et al. 2023; Lee et al. 2025; Gao et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2024). These advances improve navi-gation capability, typically under detailed, trajectory-aligned training instructions. 

Instruction-centric language interfaces. Synthetic in-struction generation scales VLN supervision (Huang et al. 2022; Kamath et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023b; Gopinathan et al. 2024), while landmark-centered generation and ULN expose the importance of specificity (Wang et al. 2022; Feng et al. 2022). Closest to our setting, VLN-Trans de-composes instructions into landmark-discriminative sub-instructions (Zhang and Kordjamshidi 2023); T-araVLN translates language for agricultural navigation (Zhao et al. 2025); and RAM rewrites observation–instruction pairs for augmentation and unseen generalization (Wei et al. 2025). These methods optimize clarity, recognizability, or training-data coverage (Wang et al. 2023a). 

Our position. TGIT never updates the UAV navigator, and it ranks candidate translations with simulator rollouts and updates only a LoRA front-end via preference learn-ing (Rafailov et al. 2023; An et al. 2023; Calandriello et al. 2024). Multimodal preference methods often align VLM re-sponses or retrain the VLA itself (Xing et al. 2025; Hung et al. 2025); TGIT instead uses original-success / weak-failure hard cases to restrict supervision to episodes where the naviga-tor has already demonstrated the required skill. Thus TGIT targets interface activation rather than policy retraining, in-struction imitation, or data augmentation.

3.2 Trajectory-Grounded Instruction Translation 

Overview: closed training–deployment loop. Figure 2 summarizes TGIT. Training first selects original-success / weak-failure hard cases. For each sampled weak input, fθ

generates N candidate commands; the frozen navigator exe-cutes every candidate; a weighted trajectory reward converts their outcomes into pairwise preferences; and DPO updates only the LoRA translator before the hard-case pool is re-sampled for the next round. At deployment, this loop col-lapses to one forward pass: a deployment-style instruction and available RGB frames enter fθ , one executable com-mand is emitted, and π flies unchanged. Simulator rewards, preference pairs, and reference trajectories are training-only signals. Section 4.3 separately tests pool construction, reward ranking, and translator backbone choices without redefining the primary protocol. Hard-case pool construction. Under a limited execu-tion budget, random sampling yields many uninformative episodes: either the navigator already fails under Orig, or Weak happens to succeed by chance. We therefore construct a compact hard-case pool Dpool . Each instance is selected such that the original dataset instruction succeeds, while the corresponding weakened human-style instruction fails un-der the same frozen agent. Writing S(π, I, E) ∈ { 0, 1} for episode success, the recoverable candidate set is 

Dpool = {(Im, I w, E) : S(π, I m, E) = 1 ,S(π, I w, E) = 0 }. (3) These original-success / weak-failure cases isolate the in-struction interface mismatch: the agent has demonstrated the required navigation ability, but the weakened command is not executable enough. In practice, we build a pool of 100 

hard cases for each UAV agent and sample K=20 cases per round, resampling across rounds to expose diverse failure modes. Failures under both Im and Iw are not used as ev-idence of a recoverable language problem. Each pair holds the route, initial condition, simulator, and policy fixed while changing only the instruction: Orig success witnesses capa-bility, whereas Weak failure isolates the interface interven-tion. Section 4.3 ablates this selection and resampling against random and fixed-subset alternatives. 

Candidate generation and execution. For each sampled instruction x ∈ Dpool , the translator generates N = 3

candidate executable commands {yi}Ni=1 conditioned on 

(x, O1: M ) under format constraints. The translator condi-tions on visual context O1: M as an input prior; Section 4.3 varies frame count (e.g., one vs. four frames) to test this prior. We use complementary decoding settings: one deter-ministic setting for stable command structure and stochastic settings for semantic variation. During training, the original dataset instruction is also included as a strong execution ref-erence, but inference uses only the human-style input and available observations. Each candidate yi is executed by the frozen UAV agent, producing a trajectory τi with success, path efficiency, and final-distance measurements. Execution is essential because text similarity cannot identify an exe-cutable command: close paraphrases may induce different behavior, while distinct surface forms may produce equally successful trajectories (Daniele, Bansal, and Walter 2017; Liang et al. 2022; Zhou, Hong, and Wu 2024).

yi ≻ yj if R(τi, T ) > R (τj , T ). (5) This transforms non-differentiable rollout outcomes into a stable supervision signal for preference learning. Unlike pure SFT on a single target phrasing, preference pairs teach the translator which of several plausible commands actually acti-vate π. Near-tied outcomes are discarded to avoid supervision from simulator noise. The weighted scalar above remains the primary TGIT reward. 

Multi-round DPO optimization. We optimize the trans-lator with Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al. 2023), using LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) to update only a small set of VLM parameters. Each round starts from and references the round-matched SFT adapter, samples 

K = 20 instructions from Dpool , generates and executes candidates, constructs preference pairs with a minimum re-ward gap, and updates fθ . Adapters are chained across rounds so later rounds refine an already grounded interface rather than restarting from the base VLM. As the translator im-proves, its candidates produce better trajectories, which in turn provide more reliable preferences for the next round— closing the feedback loop. Throughout this process, the UAV policy π is never modified; all gains come from aligning human-style inputs with the frozen agent’s executable com-mand space. Each round changes the sampled hard cases and candidates, but not the navigator or evaluation protocol. Re-sampling broadens failure coverage, while adapter chaining preserves executable patterns. Unlike general online prefer-ence collection (Calandriello et al. 2024), TGIT evaluates language actions with a fixed policy. 

Training–deployment contract. Adaptation may access simulator rollouts, reference paths, and trajectory metrics only to construct preference pairs. Evaluation removes all three: the translator receives the deployment instruction and available RGB context, emits one command, and then exits the control loop. The frozen navigator consumes that com-mand using its original controller and stopping rule. This contract prevents reward or trajectory leakage at deployment and attributes behavioral changes to the learned interface rather than to policy updates. 

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate TGIT on three frozen UAV navigation agents: OpenFly-Agent (Gao et al. 2025), CityNav-Agent (Lee et al. 2025), and AirVLN-Agent (Liu et al. 2023). All naviga-tion policies remain unchanged; only the front-end translator is trained. The translator is Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) ( r=4 , α=8 ) on language modules. For each agent, we build a hard-case pool of 100 original-success / weak-failure instructions, sample K=20 cases per round for 5 rounds, and generate N =3 candidates with visual frames. DPO starts from and references the round-matched SFT adapter, uses β=0 .1, a learning rate of 2×10 −5, and discards pairs with a reward gap below 0.05 . Adapters carry across rounds; the navigator remains fixed. These settings define the main protocol; Section 4.3 ab-lates hard-case pool construction, reward ranking, visual frames, and translator backbone as diagnostics. 

Splits and evaluation. We factor evaluation scenes by two axes used in prior aerial VLN protocol: whether the 

translator was optimized on that scene ( Selected vs. Unse-lected ), and whether the frozen agent trained on that scene (Seen vs. Unseen ) (Gao et al. 2025; Lee et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2023). Selected-Seen / Unselected-Seen / Unseen thus contain 203 /202 /404 OpenFly, 326 /180 /220 CityNav, and 

120 /192 /147 AirVLN episodes. Selected-Seen is the pool-construction scene for TGIT; Unselected-Seen is agent-seen but translator-unselected; Unseen is held out for the agent. Each closed-loop rollout lasts at most 100 agent steps, and success requires final distance below 20 m. 

Baselines and Human transfer. We compare Orig, Weak, ICL, and SFT under the Weak protocol; TGIT(W) is the pri-mary result. On OpenFly Selected-Seen, sixteen annotators provide one natural instruction per episode. Human anchors the deployment gap; Weak is the scalable, Human-informed stress protocol. We also report zero-shot TGIT(H), which only reuses the TGIT(W) adapter. 

Protocol consistency. Within each agent and split, meth-ods use the same episode set, initial conditions, rollout hori-zon, success threshold, controller, and metric implementa-tion. Only the instruction path and translator update differ. 4.2 Results 

The instruction gap is behavioral. On the same 203 

OpenFly Selected-Seen episodes and frozen navigator, SR drops from 31 .03% with Orig to 15 .27% with Weak and 

11 .33% with Human. Because the route, initial condition, controller, and evaluation remain fixed, this collapse iso-lates instruction source as the changed interface variable. The lower Human result also shows that the gap is not merely an artifact of LM weakening. Without Human-specific training, the same Weak-trained TGIT adapter raises Human-input SR from 11 .33% to 32 .51% (2.87 ×), confirming that interface activation transfers beyond the Weak proxy. Thus the de-ployment mismatch is behavioral, not only a difference in wording or length. 

Trajectory grounding recovers the interface. Direct Weak execution reaches 15 .27% SR; prompt-only ICL im-proves modestly to 19 .70% , and SFT target imitation recovers to 29 .55% but remains below Orig. TGIT reaches 37 .93% ,gaining 22 .66 points over Weak, 8.38 over SFT, and 6.90 

over Orig. The controls identify the missing supervision: ICL rewrites without agent feedback, while SFT learns one executable target without comparing alternative commands under rollout outcomes. TGIT instead executes and ranks candidates before updating only the translator. This pattern is consistent with trajectory-grounded preference learning sup-plying information beyond prompting and target imitation; Section 4.3 separately tests the hard-case and preference-design mechanisms. 

Transfer reveals a backbone-bounded regime. Beyond the pool-construction environment, TGIT raises OpenFly SR from 14 .11% to 25 .25% on Unselected-Seen and from 4.95% 

to 20 .79% on Unseen. The relative activation also trans-fers across frozen agents: CityNav improves on Selected-Seen ( 6.11% → 10 .00% ), Unselected-Seen ( 1.84% →

9.20% ), and Unseen ( 0.00% → 3.64% ); AirVLN improves on Selected-Seen ( 0.83% → 7.50% ) and Unselected-Seen (0.00% → 1.56% ). The OpenFly gains outside its 100 -case pool are inconsistent with a pool-memorization-only expla-nation, while the cross-agent gains show that the activation pattern is not specific to one navigator (Wang et al. 2025a; Anderson et al. 2021). TGIT primarily expands success cov-erage rather than jointly optimizing every navigation met-ric. Its OpenFly Selected-Seen SPL ( 24 .85% ) remains below Orig and SFT, while the high Human SPL describes only the 

11 .33% successful subset. At the agent level, CityNav and AirVLN exhibit relative recovery but low absolute SR, and every AirVLN method remains at 0.00% on Unseen. This dual pattern—transferable relative activation but backbone-bounded absolute success—defines the claim: TGIT repairs recoverable interface failures but does not supply missing navigation skill.

5 Conclusion 

We studied an instruction-interface gap in aerial VLN: frozen agents can fail under short, underspecified inputs even when they possess the required navigation skill. Human an-notations establish the mismatch, while Weak provides a controlled stress protocol. TGIT closes the interface loop through hard-case selection, trajectory-ranked preferences, and pool resampling while keeping the UAV agent frozen. It raises OpenFly Selected-Seen SR from 15 .27% to 37 .93% ,exceeding Orig ( 31 .03% ), ICL, and SFT. Limitations and future work. TGIT currently performs one-shot translation before navigation and does not revise commands online as observations or execution states change. A natural next step is latency-aware, real-time closed-loop translation that updates executable instructions during flight. Moreover, the current agents remain limited in perception and control; another direction is to directly strengthen the navigation backbone itself.
