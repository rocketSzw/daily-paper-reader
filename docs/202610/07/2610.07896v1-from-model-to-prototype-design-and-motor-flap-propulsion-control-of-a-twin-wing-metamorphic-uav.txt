# From Model to Prototype: Design and Motor-Flap Propulsion Control of a Twin-Wing Metamorphic UAV 

Anja Bosak*, Dorian Eri´ c*, Ana Milas, Stjepan Bogdan 

Abstract — This paper presents the design and control strat-egy for MetaMorpher , a metamorphic Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV), capable of both spinning-wing hover and fixed flying-wing cruise flight. Since control of the cruise configuration is comparatively well established in the literature, this paper focuses on control of the MetaMorpher in hover mode, building on the flight dynamics model and conceptual design validated in previous work. The control algorithm is implemented using one of two phase-synchronized strategies: motor propulsion, which pulses motor thrust in synchrony with the vehicle’s rotation, or flap propulsion, a novel strategy that pulses the deflection of the wing-mounted elevons. We evaluate both strategies in simulation through different flight experiments. Simulation results confirm the mathematical model and show stable reference tracking, demonstrating flap propulsion as a lightweight, decoupled alternative for hover control. Experi-mental testing validated the vertical-dynamics propulsion model against the physical prototype, demonstrating very good steady-state agreement across different configurations. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) capable of both vertical takeoff and hovering, as well as efficient long-range cruising, are needed for tasks such as power-line monitoring [1] and bridge inspection [2], which require confined launch sites and extended range. Conventional rotorcraft and fixed-wing aircraft each satisfy only one of these requirements. Metamorphic UAVs meet both, reconfiguring their airframe between hover- and cruise-optimized shapes. However, turn-ing from this concept into a flying, controllable vehicle requires an actuator layout and suitable control strategy. We address this challenge with MetaMorpher , which builds on previously developed spincopter [3], [4], an in-herently stable UAV whose twin-wing rotating structure generates lift by spinning two wings with a pair of propellers. MetaMorpher extends this platform into a metamorphic design that folds between the spinning-wing hover con-figuration and a fixed flying-wing cruise configuration. Its conceptual design and a nonlinear, segmented-aerodynamics flight dynamics model have previously been presented and validated through simulation in [5]. Implementing position control in the hover configuration, however, poses a challenge that conventional Vertical Take-Off and Landing (VTOL) controllers do not face. In this case, the aerodynamic surfaces and propulsion units rotate with the airframe, a fixed-axis control input cannot pro-duce a constant horizontal force. Instead, the control action

Conventional UAV airframes typically use a single con-figuration, trading hover capability for cruise efficiency. Metamorphic designs avoid this trade-off through approaches ranging from bio-inspired wing folding [7] to dedicated tilt-rotor [8] and tail-sitter [9] layouts. However, most of this work stops at mechanical concepts and theoretical analysis. Accurate simulation models and physical prototypes with control strategies validated through flight tests remain rare. Few review papers address the design of morphing aircraft alongside their flight control. For instance, Chu et al. [10] reviewed the broader field of morphing aircraft, covering configuration design methods, the nonlinear dynamics and aerodynamic interference introduced by wing morphing, and the flight control strategies used to address them. Authors in [11] focused more specifically on control, surveying shape, flight-dynamics, and aeroelastic control strategies for a wide range of fixed-wing morphing concepts. Harvey et al. [12] took a different angle, reviewing avian-inspired morphing in which wing sweep, dihedral, twist, and camber replace conventional control surfaces to provide pitch, roll, and yaw control, including on tailless airframes; camber-morphing trailing edges, similar to elevons, provide pitch control when actuated symmetrically and roll control when actuated asymmetrically. Across these reviews, the scope remains fixed-wing or gliding flight, while none address vehicles like MetaMorpher that transition between rotary-wing hover and fixed-wing cruise. Several hardware platforms have demonstrated metamor-phic hover-to-cruise flight. Low et al. [13], [14] developed the Transformable HOvering Rotorcraft (THOR), which in-spired MetaMorpher’s wing sizing and models the vehicle as a rigid body in each flight mode. Sufiyan et al. [9], [15] in-troduced a multimodal vehicle, jointly optimizing design and control parameters through a collaborative co-evolutionary approach. State-of-the-art control papers have also focused on specific aspects of the transition problem. Yuksek et al. [16] developed a six-degree-of-freedom nonlinear model for transition control of a tilt-rotor UAV and validated it through both simulation and a physical flight demonstration on their Wingspan

Both of MetaMorpher’s control strategies, detailed in Section IV, rely on synchronizing the control input with the vehicle’s rotational phase rather than producing a fixed control force in the body frame. This principle is closely related to cyclic pitch control on a conventional helicopter rotor, where blade pitch is varied once per revolution to redirect the rotor’s thrust in a commanded inertial direction [18]. On the spincopter, this principle was implemented entirely through differential motor thrust [3], [4]. Phase-synchronized flap deflection has also been investigated on single-wing, samara-inspired monocopters, where a trailing-edge flap is deflected within selected angular sectors of each revolution to produce horizontal control forces [19]. This work bridges these gaps by implementing MetaMor-pher’s previously validated model as a flight-ready prototype and evaluating two phase-synchronized control strategies for its hover mode. Finally, this work lays the foundation for combining motor and flap propulsion into a single control architecture and for extending phase-synchronized control across the transition between hover and cruise flight. III. M ETA MORPHER DESIGN 

A. Design Overview 

The UAV is designed to operate in both spinning-wing hover and fixed-wing cruise configurations. The transition mechanism follows a concept similar to [14], where the two wings are independently rotated by servo actuators about spanwise pivot axes. Each wing is connected to the fuselage through a one-degree-of-freedom (1-DoF) revolute joint. The pivot shafts also serve as the primary wing spars, reducing the number of structural components and the overall mechanism complexity. The final vehicle layout, shown in Fig.2, includes two independently driven wing-mounted propellers and two in-dependently actuated trailing-edge control surfaces, referred to as flaps in the spinning-wing hover configuration and as elevons in the fixed-wing cruise configuration, reflecting their differing aerodynamic role in each flight mode. This arrangement provides separate port and starboard propulsion and aerodynamic control inputs, which are used in this work to generate asymmetric forces and moments. Further details of the mechanical layout, electronic components, and wing geometry are given in the following subsections. 

B. Actuator Layout 

The wing pivot shafts pass through the central fuselage and are positioned as far forward as permitted by the wing geometry and structural requirements. This leaves sufficient volume for the battery, flight controller, and other onboard electronics while allowing the center of mass to be placed within the required range. The pivot shafts also act as the primary wing spars, such that the wing rotation mechanism does not require an additional load-bearing element. The rotation of the wing is coupled to the actuator through a pair of gears, giving the wings a ±90 ◦ range of motion. The propulsion units are positioned near the outer wing sections to increase the moment arm of the thrust force about the vehicle rotation axis. This increases the available spin-up moment in the hover configuration and the control moment available from differential thrust. However, the local tangential velocity at the propeller distance from the center of the UAV, rm, increases with radial distance from the rotation axis ωx, Vtan = rmωx. The motor position was therefore selected as a compromise between thrust moment and propeller performance under the increased local inflow velocity. The final actuator placement is shown in Fig. 2. The elevon servo actuators are installed within recessed cavities on the upper wing surface to minimize external protrusions and the associated aerodynamic drag. A mechan-ical linkage transfers the servo motion to the trailing-edge elevons. The geometry of the linkage provides an elevon deflection range of ±35 ◦ (with respect to the wing), which is used for both conventional control inputs and the asymmetric periodic actuation considered in this work. 

C. Wing Design 

The wing geometry was selected following the design procedure from [5]. The UAV has a wingspan of 0.7 m , a reference wing area of approximately 0.09 m 2, a root chord of 160 mm , and a taper ratio of 0.688 . For the estimated ready-to-fly mass of 0.45 kg , this corresponds to a target wing loading of 5 kg /m2.The cruise configuration uses the reflexed Eppler E387 airfoil, while a symmetric NACA 0010 profile is used in the hover configuration. The symmetric hover profile avoids an aerodynamic bias when one wing is inverted during the transformation. The two profiles were selected with similar outer geometry to support the wing-profile morphing mechanism. Each wing contains an independently actuated trailing-edge elevon with a span of 225 mm and a mean chord of 23 mm . The elevons are positioned with their

D. Hardware Components 

The onboard electronics consist of a custom Flight Con-troller (FC) based on the RP2040 microprocessor, two Elec-tronic Speed Controllers (ESCs), a radio receiver, and an IMU module. The flight controller gathers inertial measure-ments from the IMU and generates independent commands for the port and starboard propulsion units, wing actuators, and elevon servos. The two propulsion units are driven by ESCs running AM32 firmware using the DSHOT protocol for communication with the FC, selected to support the current requirements of the 2207 BLDC motors and to allow rapid motor-speed modulation. The flight control system was developed for the spinning flight configuration of the vehicle, in which the aerodynamic surfaces and wing-mounted propulsion system rotate together with the airframe. In this configuration, conventional fixed-axis control inputs cannot be applied directly to generate a constant force in the inertial horizontal plane. Instead, the control action must be synchronized with the rotational phase of the vehicle. Two control approaches were considered. The first uses differential modulation of the tip-mounted motors, while the second uses periodic deflection of the aerodynamic flaps. In both cases, horizontal control is achieved by apply-ing the control input only during selected portions of each rotation. 

A. Motor Pulsation 

The motor-based control system uses the two wing-mounted propellers both to support the vehicle vertically and to generate horizontal control forces. The motor commands therefore consist of a nominal thrust component together with additional contributions from the altitude, rotational-speed, and horizontal-position controllers. Altitude control is achieved by simultaneously adjusting the thrust of both motors around the nominal operating point. The altitude error, determined from the difference between the reference and measured altitude, is processed by the altitude controller to generate a thrust correction. When additional vertical force is required, the thrust of both motors is increased, while a reduction in thrust is commanded when the vehicle needs to descend. Applying the altitude correction equally to both motors changes the total thrust while maintaining the required balance between the two propulsion units. The rotational motion about the longitudinal body axis is controlled using the measured roll rate ωx. The difference between the reference and measured roll rate is processed by a PI controller. The resulting control action modifies the motor commands so that the desired spinning condition can be maintained. As already mentioned, the horizontal motion is generated using a phase-synchronized motor pulsation mechanism. The horizontal position error is first converted into a desired direction of motion in the horizontal plane of FW . This di-rection is represented by a phase angle, λ, which determines the angular location within each vehicle rotation at which the motor correction should be applied. The instantaneous rotational phase γ of the vehicle is compared with λ. A threshold-based switching law activates the horizontal motor correction only within selected angular sectors of the rotation:

C. Combined Control Strategy 

The motor and flap controllers are based on the same underlying concept, the continuous rotation of the vehicle is used as part of the control mechanism. Rather than attempting to produce a fixed body-frame control force, the control input is synchronized with the rotational phase so that the resulting force acts in a desired inertial-frame direction. In this work, the motor and flap pulsation mechanisms are developed and evaluated independently, characterizing each channel’s individual contribution to horizontal control. Our results show that flap pulsation achieves a faster response than motor pulsation (Fig. 7, Fig. 5). This suggests that the two mechanisms are not simply interchangeable but potentially complementary. Flap pulsation can be used for fast, fine-grained position corrections, and motor pulsation as an additional control for larger position changes. Combining both actuators into a single architecture that exploits this complementarity is proposed as future work. In such asystem, the motors would continue to provide the primary thrust, altitude regulation, and rotational-speed control, while horizontal control would be distributed between motor and flap pulsation according to the magnitude and urgency of the required correction. The analysis was performed in the spinning flight configuration and focused on the vertical dynamics of the vehicle and its horizontal position control capability. First, the vertical response was identified for different wing configurations. The motor and flap pulsation controllers were then evaluated separately to determine their ability to generate controlled horizontal motion while main-taining the commanded altitude.

This section validates the simulation model of Section III against the physical prototype by comparing steady-state response to a commanded throttle step change. A propeller’s thrust depends not only on rotational speed but also on the advance ratio J = V∞/(nD ), where V∞ is the inflow velocity at the propeller disk, n the rotational speed, and 

D the propeller diameter; as J increases, the thrust coef-ficient, and thus the thrust produced at a given rotational speed, decreases. The comparison is restricted to steady state because the expected mismatch, a J dependent reduction in propeller thrust, depends on the tangential velocity the propeller experiences due to the vehicle’s own spin, Vtan =

rmωx (Section III-B), which reaches a fixed value once the measured spin rate ωx settles. 

A. Experimental Setup 

Each test began at a 30% throttle baseline, matching the initial condition used in simulation, after which a step command was applied to a target between 60% and 80% in 5% increments and held until ωx reached steady state. This range was bounded by the measurement system (Optitrack) volume: below 60% the vehicle did not lift off, and above 80% it climbed out of the tracking volume before vertical ve-locity settled. Spin rate and vertical velocity were measured not only with an optical marker-tracking system, but were backed also by onboard IMU fusion. Three configurations were tested to separate wing angle and elevon effects: 30 ◦

wing angle/ 0◦ flap (A), 40 ◦ wing angle/ 0◦ flap (B), and 30 ◦

wing angle/ 15 ◦ flap (C), each swept over the full throttle range described above. The UAV performing tests in a real-world scenario is shown in Fig. 1.

VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presents the design and hover-mode control of MetaMorpher, a metamorphic UAV combining spinning-wing hover with fixed flying-wing cruise. Building on a pre-viously validated flight dynamics model, we implemented a physical prototype with independently driven wing-mounted propellers and independently actuated elevons, and devel-oped two phase-synchronized hover control strategies: motor pulsation, modulating differential thrust, and flap pulsation, a novel strategy pulsing elevon deflection while motor speed is held constant. Simulation results validated both strategies, reaching comparable final-waypoint accuracy, suggesting the two channels are complementary rather than interchangeable. Experimental testing further validated the vertical-dynamics propulsion model against the physical prototype, showing very good steady-state agreement across three wing/elevon configurations. Future work will address the propulsion model’s transient and periodic response, a prerequisite for hardware evaluation of the horizontal motion controllers in hover mode. Also, synthesis of combined motor and flap pulsation into a single architecture exploiting their complementary strengths, should be investigated, which will provide a comparison of their efficiency and control performance across configurations by closed-loop flight testing of the full controller on the physical MetaMorpher prototype.
