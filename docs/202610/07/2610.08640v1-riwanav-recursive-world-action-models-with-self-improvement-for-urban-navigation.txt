Abstract — Long-horizon urban navigation requires sequential local decisions whose errors can compound over time. Imitation learning (IL) rarely learns from failures, while physical trial-and-error reinforcement learning (RL) is costly. Action-conditioned world models can provide imagined feedback by predicting visual consequences for candidate actions. However, a frozen world model may become less reliable as the policy evolves. In this paper, we introduce RIWAN AV , a post-training framework that casts the coupled adaptation of a world model and an action model (policy) as task-specific recursive self-improvement (RSI). Each cycle alternates two updates. The world model evaluates policy actions through imagined outcomes, providing comparative feedback for group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). The improved policy then constructs a grounded self-curriculum, selecting expert-consistent action–video pairs by behavioral novelty and prediction error. The refined world model supplies feedback for the next policy update, closing the recursive self-improvement loop. Experiments show that RIWAN AV 

outperforms training baselines and prior methods, validating the proposed recursive self-improvement loop between the policy and world model. Real-world trials further demonstrate its practical applicability. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Long-horizon urban navigation requires an agent to reach a language-specified destination from street-level observations and recent motion history. At each step, the agent must translate the global goal into a local decision, recover from mistakes, and determine when to stop. Existing methods commonly learn this behavior by imitating expert trajecto-ries [1], [2]. Although imitation learning (IL) provides strong initialization, it mainly supervises successful expert actions and cannot learn from policy-induced failures, as shown in Fig. 1(a). This limitation is particularly consequential in urban navigation, where one inaccurate local motion changes the next observation and can compound over subsequent decisions [3]. Reinforcement learning (RL) can learn from such failures, but collecting long-horizon trial-and-error interaction is costly. Navigation post-training therefore needs outcome feedback without requiring every candidate action to be physically executed. Action-conditioned world models serve as learned sim-ulators for policy improvement. Navigation world models (NWM) [4] and recent world action models [3], [5], [6] predict future observations conditioned on candidate actions. Their imagined outcomes allow a policy to compare local decisions before execution. However, a world model used for

A. Real-World Navigation 

Real-world navigation policies are learned from diverse robot trajectories and goal-conditioned supervision [9], [10], [11]. CityWalker [1] scales urban navigation learning with large collections of walking and driving videos. U RBAN -NAV [2] aligns city-scale human trajectories with landmark-grounded language. OmniVLA [12] learns a shared navigation policy across image, language, and mixed goal modalities. Other works explore generative guidance: NoMaD [11] uses a goal-masked diffusion policy, whereas VENTURA [13] generates instruction-conditioned visual paths for downstream control. These methods primarily improve navigation through data, supervision, and policy design. Our work instead focuses on post-training. 

B. World Models for Policy Learning 

World models support planning and policy learning through learned future dynamics [14], [15]. PlaNet [16] and Dreamer [17] established latent imagination for policy learning, with later works scaling to broader domains [18], [19] and high-dimensional visual observations [20], [21]. Recent world models extend to navigation and embodied control [22], [23], while increasingly connect visual prediction with policy optimization [3], [24]. NWM [4] ranks navigation trajectories using controllable video prediction, whereas Fast-WAM [6] jointly models video and actions to improve policy learning without explicit future generation. Rather than treating the world model as a fixed source of imagined feedback, we study how the world model and policy can continually adapt to one another as policy behavior evolves. 

C. Recursive Self-Improvement 

Recursive self-improvement traditionally studies sys-tems that improve their own problem-solving process [7]. POET [25] co-evolves environment–agent pairs, generates new challenges, and transfers solutions across environments. Model-based policy learning faces a related problem at the level of learned dynamics. Uncertainty-aware prediction and short rollouts reduce exploitation of model errors [14], [15], while conservative objectives penalize uncertain transi-tions [26], [27]. PDML [28] adapts model learning toward the current policy distribution. Other approaches prioritize ex-perience through learning progress, novelty, or curiosity [29], [30], [31]. In this paper, we connect these directions: the world model improves the policy through imagined feedback, and the evolving policy guides the next world-model update. This closed loop enables the two components to improve one another recursively. III. M ETHOD 

We introduce RIWAN AV , a post-training framework that recursively couples a navigation policy with an action-conditioned world model. As shown in Fig. 2, each cycle alternates between policy optimization and world-model adaptation. First, the current world model predicts the visual outcomes of candidate actions, providing a basis for comparing actions and improving the policy. Second, the improved policy generates candidate actions, and those consistent with expert motion are paired with recorded future frames to build the policy-induced replay pool. The grounded self-curriculum prioritizes these pairs by behavioral novelty and current prediction error for world-model adaptation. The refined world model then supplies imagined feedback for the next policy update, closing the recursive self-improvement loop.

This reciprocal exchange closes the recursive self-improvement loop. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We test our RIWAN AV through three questions. First, can a learned visual world model improve the navigation policy beyond policy-only training? Second, does policy-guided world-model refinement improve over a frozen world model? Third, does the world model become more accurate on behaviors from the improved policy? Controlled comparisons, ablations, and world-model analyses address these questions in sequence. A physical-robot deployment evaluates transfer beyond offline benchmarking. 

A. Experimental Setup a) Dataset and evaluation: We evaluate on the official train, test-seen, and test-unseen splits of U RBAN NAV [2]. All training frameworks receive the same recent-frame, pose-history, and language goals and predict eight local waypoints plus a stop probability. Every framework uses the same data split, episode list, trajectory mapping, and 3-m success radius. Unless otherwise stated, results are means and standard deviations over three random seeds. Success Rate (SR) measures final goal attainment, Oracle Success Rate (OSR) records whether any visited pose reaches the goal region, and Navigation Error (NE) measures final goal distance. Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) evaluates path efficiency [36]; normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW) and success-weighted nDTW (SDTW) evaluate trajectory fidelity [37]. Higher values are better except for NE. 

b) Baselines: The controlled comparison fixes the policy architecture [2], initialization, data, interface, and evalua-tion protocol while changing the post-training framework. Imitation learning [2] is the common policy trained on expert trajectories. PPO [35] and GRPO [8] are policy-only baselines that optimize the same navigation objective without action-conditioned visual feedback. PPO-WM and GRPO-WM use the corresponding policy optimizer with imagined consequences from a frozen C OSMOS [32]. RIWAN AV uses GRPO-based policy improvement and additionally adapts COSMOS with the proposed grounded self-curriculum. 

c) Implementation details: The policy predicts eight waypoints over eight seconds and compares four candidates at each state; C OSMOS predicts 17 frames at 2 fps from 16 relative-pose actions. Policy training uses a learning rate of 

10 −5 and clipping range η = 0 .20 . The self-curriculum uses expert-consistency threshold qmin = 0 .35 and equal novelty and prediction-error weights λN = λE = 0 .50 . The world model is updated every 500 policy steps using at most 500 replay examples. Training runs on four NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Real-world deployment uses a custom-built mobile robotic platform equipped with a forward-facing camera that provides RGB observations.

B. Comparative Experiments 1) Comparison with Training Frameworks: Table I com-pares all training frameworks under the same policy architec-ture, initialization, data, and evaluation protocol. This con-trolled study tests whether world-model adaptation adds value beyond the choice of policy optimizer and the availability of imagined feedback. Policy-only post-training provides limited gains from the shared imitation-learned initialization. On test-unseen, PPO changes SR/SPL by +0 .06% / − 0.38% , whereas GRPO improves them by 2.75% /2.46% . Frozen-world-model feed-back produces a larger change: PPO-WM improves SR/SPL over PPO by 7.11% /6.79% , and GRPO-WM improves them over GRPO by 9.68% /7.60% . These controlled comparisons isolate imagined visual consequences as a useful training signal even before world-model adaptation. Closing the recursive feedback loop provides a further gain over frozen world-model feedback. Relative to GRPO-WM, RIWAN AV improves SR/SPL by 6.57% /5.60% on test-seen and 7.94% /6.50% on test-unseen, achieving the best performance across all reported metrics on both splits. On test-unseen, it also reduces NE from 3.3314 to 2.0429 and raises nDTW, SDTW, and OSR from 87.77%/77.62%/81.01% to 91.95%/85.51%/89.37%. The agreement across success, path efficiency, route fidelity, and oracle success indicates that policy-guided world-model adaptation improves the complete rollout rather than only its stopping decision.

C. Ablation Study 

We evaluate world-model updates, policy-induced replay, and the grounded self-curriculum, as shown in Table III. World-model updates use recorded expert action–video pairs by default (static expert replay). Policy-induced replay instead uses expert-consistent policy actions paired with recorded future frames. The grounded self-curriculum prioritizes these pairs by behavioral novelty and prediction error; otherwise, they are sampled uniformly. With static expert replay, SR and SPL are 2.08% and 1.37% lower than those of the frozen-world-model baseline. Compared with static expert replay, policy-induced replay with uniform sampling improves SR and SPL by 6.60% and 5.39%, respectively. This shows the benefit of using actions proposed by the improved policy for world-model training. Adding the grounded self-curriculum to policy-induced replay further improves SR and SPL by 3.42% and 2.48%. This gain supports prioritizing expert-consistent samples by behavioral novelty and prediction error. Combining policy-induced replay with the grounded self-curriculum improves SR and SPL by 7.94% and 6.50% over the frozen-world-model baseline. These results show that updating the world model with actions from the improved policy yields better navigation performance than freezing it or training it only on expert data.

E. Real-World Deployment 

To assess real-world applicability, we deploy the policy trained with RIWAN AV on a mobile robot in urban navigation scenarios. Given a natural-language instruction and onboard visual observations, the policy predicts waypoints to guide the robot toward the specified goal. Figure 5 shows the resulting navigation sequences across different route layouts and lighting conditions. These qualitative trials demonstrate that the learned policy can guide a physical robot toward language-specified landmarks, supporting the practical feasibility of RIWAN AV beyond offline evaluation. 

F. Discussion and Limitations 

The controlled comparisons and ablations clarify where RIWAN AV gains arise. Imagined world-model feedback improves navigation beyond policy-only training, and policy-guided world-model adaptation provides further gains over a frozen world model. While these results support the recursive design, its current implementation remains task-specific and relies on a fixed offline dataset. Recorded futures can supervise only policy actions close to expert motion, limiting counterfactual exploration without new interaction data. Adapting a large video world model also increases memory and computation. Although real-world trials demonstrate practical feasibility, broader evaluations across cities, environments, and robot embodiments are needed. V. C ONCLUSION
