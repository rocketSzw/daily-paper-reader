# NMPP: Nonlinear Model Predictive Planning for Agile UAV Flight in Cluttered Environments 

Ondˇ rej Proch´ azka, Juraj Pauko, Robert Pˇ eniˇ cka, Martin Saska 

Abstract — Flying a quadrotor through a cluttered environ-ment requires not only planning a collision-free reference trajectory based on perceived obstacles, but the reference also needs to be dynamically feasible and within the actuation limits of the vehicle, so that the controller can track it precisely. Existing methods either optimize a smooth polynomial inside a convex corridor, which limits agility, or treat obstacles as soft costs traded against tracking performance. We propose a Nonlinear Model Predictive Planning (NMPP) that imposes perceived obstacles as hard geometric constraints and hands a full-state reference to an obstacle-blind SE (3) controller. Our planner achieves a 58– 67 % lower position RMSE than a linear Model Predictive Control trajectory planner and a 41– 70 % lower RMSE than a polynomial trajectory planner. It also completes all forest flights with up to 9.5 m s −1 speed without collisions, and achieves 86 % flight success rate under a more aggressive speed profile where a state-of-the-art planner has only 26 % success rate. The real-world deployment showed reliable execution flying up to 5.5 m s −1 in an unknown cluttered environment.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Multirotor Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are increas-ingly deployed in missions that require flight through clut-tered and initially unknown environments, including search and rescue [1] and infrastructure inspections [2]. In such missions, the achievable flight speed determines how much of the environment can be covered within the vehicle’s limited endurance, which motivates operating the platform close to its dynamic limits. Flying fast between obstacles requires a reference trajectory that is not only collision-free but also respects the UAV’s dynamics and actuation limits. The obstacles are perceived by the onboard sensors only during the flight, so the trajectory has to be planned online. The trajectory is often generated with simplified vehicle dynamics, causing a mismatch between the planned and flown trajectory [3]. Moreover, collision avoidance and dynamic feasibility are commonly handled in separate stages, where a collision-free path is first found in a map and then converted into a trajectory for the controller. Thus, the path ignores the dynamics and the trajectory ignores the obstacles. This paper addresses the generation of such a trajectory in a single onboard optimization, which respects the UAV

The planner relies only on onboard sensing and has to return a solution within a few milliseconds of each planning period. The result is continuously handed to an SE (3) geometric controller [4] as a full-state reference, so that the controller can follow it without deviating into the obstacles it was planned around. Meeting these requirements in one optimization is difficult for three reasons. First, the free space around obstacles is nonconvex, so imposing the obstacles as hard constraints makes the problem nonconvex and prone to local minima and infeasibility. Second, the sensor returns a variable and unbounded number of obstacles, while the problem has to keep a fixed size for its computation time to stay pre-dictable. Third, a solution that is feasible at the discrete prediction nodes can still intersect an obstacle between two of them. Existing work addresses these difficulties in two ways. Corridor-based polynomial optimization restricts the trajectory to a convex subset of the free space, which removes the nonconvexity and allows online computation [5], [6]. However, the smooth polynomial representation keeps the vehicle from flying close to its dynamic limits. Placing the avoidance inside the control loop instead preserves the reaction to what is currently perceived [7], [8], [9], but the obstacles are then expressed as soft costs, so that the avoidance is traded against tracking rather than guaranteed. To this end, we propose Nonlinear Model Predictive Planning (NMPP), which uses Nonlinear Model Predictive arXiv:2610.08695v1 [cs.RO] 6 Oct 2026 Control (NMPC) as a trajectory planner that generates a full-state reference for a separate controller rather than actuator commands. Each optimization is initialized from the previously generated prediction rather than from the noisy state estimate, which allows imposing the obstacle constraint exactly. The accumulated point cloud is converted into ellipsoids, of which a fixed number enters the problem, keeping its size constant regardless of the scene. The hard constraint is complemented by a soft obstacle penalty and a softened acceleration-level Higher-Order Control Barrier Function (HOCBF) condition [10]. The full obstacle set validates the trajectory between prediction nodes. To enforce actuation limits efficiently, the NMPC uses the thrust rate as its control input. The thrust and angular-acceleration limits thus become linear constraints. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first planner that imposes LiDAR-perceived obstacles as hard constraints on the full quadrotor dynamics with thrust dynamics and runs onboard. In simulation, NMPP is compared with two trajectory planners, one based on linear Model Predictive Controller (MPC) [11] and one based on polynomial trajectories [12]. All three provide their reference to the same unmodified 

SE (3) controller [4], so the reference is the only component that differs. Across five constraint profiles with speed limits, NMPP achieves a 58– 67 % lower position Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) than the linear MPC trajectory planner and a 41– 70 % lower RMSE than the polynomial trajectory planner. The obstacle avoidance is evaluated in a Poisson forest against SUPER [6], with both planners flying in the same world towards the same goal. NMPP completed all flights without collision at speeds up to 9.5 m s −1 (super fast 

profile), whereas the success rate of SUPER dropped to 70 % 

under that profile. Under a more aggressive profile, the two planners reached the goal in 86 % and 26 % of fifty flights, respectively. Methods for autonomous UAV navigation in cluttered en-vironments range from reactive collision avoidance to global, optimization-based, and learning-based trajectory planning. Reactive methods compute avoidance commands directly from the current perception data. Artificial Potential Fields (APF) represent obstacles through repulsive potentials [13], while Velocity Obstacles identify control velocities that may lead to a future collision [14]. Extensions such as ORCA support reciprocal collision avoidance between mul-tiple agents [15]. Reciprocal velocity constraints have also been integrated into NMPC for mutual collision avoidance in agile UAV flight [16]. These approaches are computationally efficient, but their local decisions can lead to local minima and usually rely on simplified vehicle dynamics. Global motion planners instead search for a collision-free path in a representation of the environment. Graph-search and sampling-based algorithms, such as A*, RRT, and RRT*, can find paths through complex obstacle configurations [17]. The resulting path is commonly used to further plan a smooth trajectory that can be tracked by the UAV. Minimum jerk/snap polynomial trajectories are widely used for this purpose because the differential flatness of a quadrotor allows smooth position trajectories to be mapped to reference attitude and thrust [18], [19]. Efficient polynomial repre-sentations further enable the generation of long trajectories with many segments [12]. Minimum-time formulations based on a point-mass model exploit the maneuverability of the UAV more effectively than polynomial trajectories, at the cost of a reference that is only partially feasible for the real vehicle [3]. However, these methods generally require predefined waypoints or a collision-free corridor and do not themselves determine how to avoid newly perceived obstacles. Optimization-based planners incorporate smoothness, dy-namic feasibility, and collision avoidance into a single trajectory-planning problem. Corridor-based methods such as GCOPTER [5] efficiently optimize polynomial trajectories while enforcing smoothness-based formulations. Receding safe corridors can also be constructed incrementally along the flight, so that high-speed smooth trajectories are planned within them [20]. The corridor, however, restricts the trajec-tory to a convex subset of the free space, so the motion that can be produced is limited by how the corridor was carved rather than by the capability of the vehicle. MPC provides an alternative in which the future vehicle motion is optimized over a receding horizon. It naturally supports state, input, and actuation constraints and has there-fore been applied to UAV trajectory tracking and collision avoidance [11]. An NMPC controller that integrates the dynamics of the low-level flight controller was shown to reduce the tracking error on agile references [21]. There, the optimization is placed at the controller level, where it pro-duces the commands for the actuators, whereas the method proposed here places it at the planner level, where it produces a reference for a separate controller. The CIAO and CIAO* methods, presented in [22], [23], respectively, formulate collision avoidance through convex inner approximations of free space, providing recursively feasible predictive motion planning in static and predictable dynamic environments. The principal difficulty is that a nonlinear geometric constraint has to be introduced for every obstacle that the optimization accounts for, so the size of the problem grows with the number of obstacles that are taken into account. When the environment is measured online, that number is neither known in advance nor bounded, which is what the following approaches, and NMPP have to address. Several planners therefore reduce or avoid dense volu-metric mapping. NanoMap stores a local history of depth measurements and performs uncertainty-aware proximity queries without fusing all measurements into a global voxel-map [24]. EGO-Planner removes the need for an Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF) by extracting obstacle in-formation only when the optimized trajectory intersects an occupied region [25]. SUPER generates polyhedral flight corridors directly from LiDAR point clouds and optimizes an exploratory and a backup minimum-snap polynomial trajectory within them, to distinguish high-speed progress from motion guaranteed to remain in observed free space [6]. These methods substantially reduce mapping overhead, but collision avoidance is still performed through path search, corridor construction, and polynomial trajectory optimiza-tion. A different line of work removes the modular pipeline altogether and learns a mapping from sensor data directly to control commands, which avoids the latency accumulated across mapping, planning and control [26], [27]. These methods embed little prior knowledge about the controlled vehicle, so a change of the platform or its parameters requires retraining on large amounts of data. Another group of methods integrates perception directly into predictive control. Local point clouds can be queried directly using KD-trees to construct obstacle costs inside a point-mass MPC formulation [28]. Point-cloud constraints can be incorporated into Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based MPC by selecting risk points close to the predicted motion [7]. For quadrotors, multiple obstacle constraints can be aggregated into a composite CBF and enforced by a high-rate safety filter operating on a third-order dy-namic model [8]. Alternatively, Geometric Model Predictive Path Integral (GMPPI) approach sampled UAV rollouts di-rectly into depth images [9] and evaluates collision without constructing an explicit geometric map. These approaches demonstrate the benefits of connecting local perception with predictive control, but they either simplify the vehicle dy-namics, apply collision avoidance as a separate safety filter, or represent obstacles only through soft collision costs. In contrast, NMPP imposes the LiDAR-perceived obstacles as hard constraints on the full quadrotor dynamics with thrust dynamics, without restricting the trajectory to a convex corridor. A fixed number of ellipsoids keeps the problem size constant regardless of the scene, and the planner runs onboard while providing a full-state reference. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider the problem of online trajectory planning for a quadrotor flying toward one or a sequence of reference waypoints in an initially unknown environment. At each replanning instant, the objective is to generate a finite-horizon trajectory that progresses toward the active reference while respecting the nonlinear quadrotor dynamics and the physical limitations of the UAV. In addition, the generated trajectory must remain collision-free with respect to obstacles observed by the onboard LiDAR sensor.

C. Collision Avoidance 

The collision-avoidance method connects local LiDAR perception directly with the NMPC trajectory planner. The main difficulty is that a point cloud contains thousands of points and an unpredictable number of obstacles, whereas the NMPC must retain a fixed problem size to provide predictable onboard computation. Therefore, the measured environment is first converted into a compact set of el-lipsoidal obstacles. A fixed number of the most relevant obstacles is included in the optimization, while the complete perceived obstacle set is retained for subsequent trajectory validation. 1) Point-Cloud Processing: Let P =

{ξ1, . . . , ξ Np }, ξi ∈ R3 denote an accumulated LiDAR point cloud expressed in the static planning frame. Points corresponding to the UAV body and ground are removed, and the cloud is restricted to a local heading-aligned Range Of Interest (ROI) around the vehicle. The onboard Livox MID-360 uses a non-repetitive scanning pattern. To obtain a more complete representation of obstacle surfaces, the consecutive point clouds are accumulated over non-overlapping 0.20 s windows to obtain denser obstacle coverage. The cloud P is downsampled using a range-adaptive voxel grid [30]. A fine resolution is retained near the UAV, where accurate obstacle boundaries are important, while increas-ingly coarse resolutions are used farther away. Euclidean clustering then partitions the processed cloud into 

C = {C 1, . . . , CNc }, Cm ⊆ P , (11) where Cm denotes an obstacle cluster.

E. Implementation Details 

The planner is integrated into the open-source UAV system of [31] and runs at 100 Hz . The horizon consists of N = 28 

shooting nodes spanning 2.0 s , distributed as five steps of 

10 ms , five of 50 ms , five of 80 ms and thirteen of 100 ms .The number of obstacle slots is fixed at Nobs = 15 when the solver is generated, so the dimensions of the problem do not change during the flight. Each iteration is initialized by sampling the stored prediction at the elapsed time. The opti-mization and the reference output are decoupled. The solver runs on its own timer, while the command passed to the controller is produced at every control step by interpolating the stored prediction between the two nodes surrounding its current age, and the prediction is discarded once it grows older than the horizon, after which the tracker falls back to commanding the current vehicle state until a new solution arrives. V. E XPERIMENTS AND RESULTS 

A. Experimental methodology 

This section evaluates NMPP in simulation and real-world experiments. First, the tracking accuracy of the generated reference is compared with a linear MPC trajectory planner [11] and a polynomial trajectory planner [12]. Collision avoidance is then evaluated in a simulated forest. Finally, the complete system is deployed in the real UAV platform. In every simulated comparison the evaluated planners provide their reference to the same unmodified SE (3) controller [4], so that the comparison isolates the reference planner, and each configuration is flown ten times, or fifty times under the agile profile. All flights are performed under one of the constraint profiles of Table I, which are enforced by the underlying UAV system. The profiles are identical for all evaluated planners. The planner’s thrust and attitude limits are fixed and do not change with the profile.

C. Real-world experiments 

NMPP is deployed on a custom-built quadrotor with a take-off mass of 3.25 kg , based on a Readytosky Mark4 10-inch V2 frame with a wheelbase of 427 mm . The propulsion consists of four iFlight XING-E 3314 900 kV motors with three-blade Gemfan 1045-3 propellers, driven by a Skystars Jupiter 80 A BL32 electronic speed controller. The low-level attitude-rate loop runs on a Holybro Pixhawk 6C mini flight controller with the PX4 firmware, which is commanded by the same UAV system [31] running on a Jetson Orin NX with an A603 carrier board. State estimation fuses the onboard Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) with a Holybro F9P Heli-cal Real-time Kinematics (RTK) Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) receiver and the obstacle measurements are provided by a Livox Mid-360 LiDAR. The real-world experiments consist of ten flights through the approximately 40 m obstacle course under the fast con-straint profile. The experiments were conducted in strong and gusty wind, which disturbed the lightweight pylons as well as the UAV. Consequently, the obstacle boundaries were not perfectly stationary during individual flights, which introduced additional perception uncertainty not present in the simulation. The aggregated real-world results are summarized in Ta-ble V and the resulting flown trajectories are shown in Fig. 5. On the onboard computer the mean solution time rises to 

13 .5 ms , above the 10 ms period of the planner loop, so a newly optimized trajectory is not available at every control step.
