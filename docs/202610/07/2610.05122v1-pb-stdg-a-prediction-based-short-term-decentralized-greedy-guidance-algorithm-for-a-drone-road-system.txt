Abstract 

In recent years, Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) or drones have been increasingly adopted in urban environments for applications such as parcel delivery, infrastructure inspection, emergency response, and drone light shows. A non-negligible issue is how to manage the increasing number of drones operated by di ff erent entities, to enable them to cooperatively avoid potential colli-sions and determine conflict-free short-term flight paths in urban airspace. This paper presents a Prediction-Based Short-Term Decentralized Greedy (PB-STDG) guidance algorithm for a struc-tured Drone Road System (DRS). PB-STDG extends the original STDG algorithm by introduc-ing a prediction mechanism that enables drones to anticipate the decisions of neighboring drones using additional information shared in beacon packets, aiming to address the over-conservative behavior observed in the STDG algorithm. Several simulation scenarios are used to evaluate and compare the proposed algorithm with STDG. The results show that PB-STDG improves tra ffi ceffi ciency while maintaining a safety level comparable to that of STDG. 

Keywords: Drone, Congestion Control, Guidance and Navigation, Road System, Wireless Communication 

1. Introduction 

Continuous advances in drones, or unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV), technology have signifi-cantly expanded the range of drone applications, attracting growing interest from both academia and industry. Although drones were initially developed for military operations [14, 6], their ap-plications have gradually expanded into a wide range of civilian and commercial fields, such as arXiv:2610.05122v1 [cs.RO] 4 Oct 2026 aerial photography [12, 25, 32], emergency response [26], and infrastructure inspection [15, 24]. More recently, drone applications have expanded into delivery and entertainment. Commercial drone delivery services are now being used for last-mile parcel distribution by companies such as Amazon Prime Air [3] and Google Wing [31]. Food delivery by drones has also entered commer-cial operation in cities including Shenzhen, China, where Meituan operates urban drone delivery routes [33]. Beyond delivery, large-scale drone light shows have become a popular feature of public celebrations, such as the drone show program at Darling Harbour in Sydney [17], where coordinated drone swarms are deployed for regular night-time performances. With the growth of these drone applications, the number of deployed drones is continuously increasing [8]. These drones generally share the same low-altitude airspace, particularly in urban environments. Unlike traditional ground tra ffi c systems, most current drone operations are con-ducted in an unstructured and free-flight manner. Each drone independently plans its trajectory based on its own algorithms and available information. Although such an approach provides flexibility, it becomes increasingly di ffi cult to maintain safety and manage large-scale drone op-erations involving di ff erent operators and service providers. Conflicts between competing flight paths may lead to tra ffi c congestion or even collisions as the number of drones increases, which can degrade overall system e ffi ciency and pose risks to public safety. To address this challenge, several studies on structured airspace management have been pro-posed, aiming to organize low-altitude airspace into predefined aerial structures and constrain drone movements within them. A representative example is the NASA drone tra ffi c management concept [11]. In this framework, the low-altitude airspace is organized into multiple layers based on altitude. High-speed fixed-wing UAVs are typically assigned to higher layers, while low-speed rotary-wing UAVs operate in lower layers. Within each layer, the airspace is further structured into di ff erent types of aerial structures, including sky-corridors, sky-tubes, and sky-lanes. Drones are allowed to fly freely within sky-corridors, whereas sky-tubes impose directional constraints on movement. Sky-lanes provide an additional level of restriction by regulating lateral motion, preventing drones from cruising in parallel within the same lane. Several studies have explored layer-based approaches for drone tra ffi c management, using di ff erent altitude layers to regulate drone movement and heading directions [7, 27, 19]. Other studies have proposed more structured drone road systems inspired by ground transportation net-2works, including corridor-based and lane-level structures [18, 29]. Building on these concepts, [22] proposes a Drone Road System framework consisting of structured roads and ramps. All road segments are designed to be unidirectional, with a defined number of lanes and an allowed speed range, to support safe and predictable drone movement. The system can also be encoded in XML format, enabling flexible updates and e ffi cient distribution across di ff erent platforms. While drone tra ffi c management systems such as drone road systems provide a structured framework for organizing low-altitude airspace, conflicts between drones can still occur within the constrained network, particularly under high-density tra ffi c conditions. In such environments, drones may compete for optimal routes, and di ff erent types of conflicts may arise due to speed di ff erences or directional changes during navigation. Therefore, e ff ective drone guidance and collision avoidance mechanisms remain essential. A large body of research has been devoted to UAV navigation and collision avoidance in free-flight environments. These include approaches such as [9] and [30], which determine short-term collision-free paths for UAVs in environments containing obstacles and other dynamic con-straints. The Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) approach [1] iteratively updates drone positions based on individual and global best solutions. Another widely studied approach is Deep Rein-forcement Learning (DRL) [10, 2, 16], which learns drone control policies through interaction with the environment. These methods typically use sensory inputs such as images, video streams, or onboard sensor data to generate control actions via deep neural networks. By optimizing a re-ward function, drones can learn collision-free navigation in dynamic and complex environments. Specifically, several studies have investigated safe guidance and collision avoidance algo-rithms for drone delivery scenarios. In [20], three collision avoidance strategies are proposed to manage large numbers of drones delivering goods to random destinations in urban environments. Similarly, [5] presents a centralized navigation approach for a fleet of drones to achieve safe point-to-point parcel delivery missions. In addition, [4] introduces two centralized algorithms to ensure safe delivery operations within a simplified two-layer airspace structure. However, these approaches su ff er from several limitations: (i) they often rely on global in-formation of other agents, (ii) they require multiple types of onboard sensors, and (iii) they are primarily designed for free-flight environments and cannot be directly adapted to structured airspace systems. In the large-scale drone tra ffi c system we focus on, where drones are owned 3by di ff erent operators and perform diverse tasks, these limitations become more critical. Specif-ically, global coordination is di ffi cult to achieve in practice, as it is unrealistic for each drone to obtain or maintain global knowledge of all other drones in the city-scale airspace. Moreover, di ff erent navigation algorithms across di ff erent drones further increase system uncertainty, since individual drones are unable to accurately predict the behavior of others under inconsistent or un-known algorithms. They will also make di ff erent assumptions about the available sensors, and in this paper, we make very minimal assumptions, requiring only GPS and wireless communication. Based on these challenges and assumptions, a research gap can be identified in the guid-ance of large-scale drone tra ffi c under limited coordination. Existing approaches often rely on assumptions about the available sensing, communication, or coordination capabilities of drones. However, in a large-scale drone road system, drones may be operated by di ff erent manufacturers or service providers and may therefore have limited ability to coordinate with one another. There is therefore a need for a guidance approach that can manage interactions among a large num-ber of independently operating drones without relying on extensive coordination or additional sensing capabilities. To address this research gap, a baseline guidance algorithm for structured drone road systems is required that can be applied to di ff erent types of drones while relying on minimal information and limited sensing capabilities. In our previous studies [21, 23], STDG was proposed to address drone route navigation and collision avoidance challenges on the standardized Drone Road Sys-tem introduced in [22]. The STDG algorithm relies only on position and velocity information exchanged between drones through beacon packets, and provides short-term, coordination-free, and decentralized guidance. Drones periodically execute STDG to make short-term lane and speed decisions based on the current tra ffi c conditions and potential collision risks. Previous simulation results for STDG revealed a key limitation: significant speed loss when avoiding potential collisions. Since the algorithm is coordination-free (i.e., drones only exchange beacon packets, with no other form of coordination) and only position and velocity information is shared, it is di ffi cult for drones to accurately estimate or predict the decisions of others. As a result, lane switching may be suppressed when multiple neighboring drones simultaneously select the same lane to switch. In such cases, drones tend to remain in their current lanes and may further reduce their speed or even come to a complete stop until the surrounding tra ffi c4conditions change. This behavior significantly degrades tra ffi c throughput and system e ffi ciency. In our previous study, the algorithm settings that allow some potential risks to be disregarded result in an increase in average speed from around 7 m /s to 10 m /s, approaching the preferred speed of 12.5 m /s, with only a slight increase in the collision rate (i.e., from 1 to 5 collisions among 150,000 drones). The results show that STDG has room to improve its average speed, as a substantial speed increase can be achieved by slightly relaxing its treatment of potential collision risks with only a small increase in actual collisions. This motivates the prediction of neighboring drones’ future actions to better distinguish po-tential risks from likely collisions, thus allowing drones to make more e ff ective lane-switching and speed-selection decisions without unnecessary stops or slowdowns and avoiding additional collisions that may occur in such situations. In this paper, we focus on designing a prediction-based extension to the previous STDG guidance algorithm. The proposed approach extends the beacon structure by including additional safety information, which allows drones to predict the movement decisions of their neighbors over the next short period. This capability helps reduce situations where drones tend to remain in their current lanes and encourages necessary lane changes, either to overtake slower predecessors or to move towards their target lanes without prolonged stopping. Extensive simulations are conducted using the same simulation framework and largely the same experimental configuration as the original STDG study. The results show that, under the same tra ffi c scenario, the proposed prediction-based algorithm substantially reduces the speed loss while maintaining a close-to-zero collision rate. Additional simulations are performed un-der di ff erent road-system configurations and algorithm parameter settings to comprehensively evaluate PB-STDG against the original STDG algorithm. The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. 

• We propose PB-STDG, a prediction-based extension of the original STDG algorithm that enables drones to predict the movement decisions of neighboring drones. The proposed approach reduces the tendency of multiple drones to remain in their current lanes during concurrent lane switching and, in the tested complex DRS, increases the average speed from 10.28 m /s to 12.18 m /s while maintaining a close-to-zero collision rate. 5• We design an extended beacon structure and an event-driven beacon transmission mecha-nism that provide the guidance algorithm with additional safety information for predicting the decisions of neighboring drones, with a modest increase in beacon packet size. 

• We conduct extensive simulations under di ff erent drone densities and algorithm configu-rations to evaluate the proposed algorithm and compare it with the STDG algorithm. The results demonstrate the e ff ectiveness of the proposed approach across di ff erent tra ffi c con-ditions and algorithm settings. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 briefly introduces the DRS adopted in this work and reviews the baseline STDG algorithm on which PB-STDG is built. Section 3 describes the system settings and assumptions, including the wireless communica-tion and networking settings, beacon content, and beacon transmission mechanism. Section 4 presents PB-STDG in detail. Section 5 describes the simulation assumptions and configurations and evaluates PB-STDG against STDG through extensive simulation studies. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper and discusses future research directions. 

2. Background 

The prediction-based algorithm proposed in this paper is built upon the drone road system (DRS) [22] and the Short-Term Decentralized Greedy (STDG) algorithm [23] developed in pre-vious work. 

2.1. Drone Road System 

For large-scale drone tra ffi c management, the drone road system introduces a common frame-work to construct predefined routes. This framework simplifies tra ffi c management and collision avoidance by making the movements of individual drones more predictable. The predefined road system can be represented in an XML file, which simplifies both road network updates and the transmission of updated information to drones currently cruising within the system. Each drone road system consists of one or multiple roads and zero or multiple ramps. Both roads and ramps are unidirectional, meaning that drones within the same road or ramp follow the same travel direction. Road is the primary component of the system and consists of one central 6lane and multiple parallel lanes arranged in a hexagonal grid topology. Each lane is assigned a unique identifier ( i, j) and can have up to six neighboring lanes (shown in Figure 1), with the identifiers ( i + 1, j), ( i − 1, j), ( i, j + 1), ( i, j − 1), ( i + 1, j − 1), and ( i − 1, j + 1). Since roads may be curved, an arc-length parameter s is introduced to represent the relative position of a drone along a lane. The value of s represents the distance traveled by the drone from the beginning of the lane along the lane. 

> Figure 1: Left: Simple lane arrangement example, identifier (0,0) is the central lane of the road. Right: Example of position-cost calculation.

Ramp is a special type of road that consists of a single lane and is used either to connect two roads or to provide additional entry and exit access to the drone road system. When constructing a ramp, one or two connection points are defined on existing road lanes. If only one connection point is specified, the ramp is referred to as an on /off -ramp, where the other end serves as either an entry or exit of the system. Otherwise, the ramp connects two lanes and is referred to as a connecting ramp. During its lifetime in the system, a drone first enters the network through either a road entry point or an on-ramp, and its end-to-end path planning is done in advance. In this study, we assume that the plan is never altered afterwards. It then navigates through the system by moving along lanes and may perform lane switching to overtake slower drones or to reach connected lanes leading to other roads or ramps. In this process, a drone can only directly switch to one of its neighboring lanes and then repeat the lane-switching maneuver until it reaches its target lane. Finally, the drone reaches its destination road or o ff -ramp and exits the system at the corresponding endpoint. 72.2. Short-Term Decentralized Greedy Algorithm 

The Short-Term Decentralized Greedy (STDG) algorithm serves as a baseline guidance method for the drone road system introduced in Section 2.1. STDG ensures that drones can successfully complete their trips within the road system while providing essential functionalities such as col-lision avoidance, lane-switching, and speed-selection. The algorithm generates short-term deci-sions based on the current local tra ffi c situation over a limited time horizon. This enables drones to overtake slower neighbors and progress toward target connection points without missing them. The inputs of STDG consist primarily of the position and velocity of the current drone and those of neighboring drones, obtained through periodic beacon transmissions. The algorithm is fully decentralized and does not require any explicit coordination beyond beacon exchange. At the start of the algorithm, a drone considers its current lane and all neighboring lanes as candidate lanes, and evaluates a weighted cost function for each candidate. The lane with the minimum cost is selected as the output lane. If the selected lane di ff ers from the current lane, the drone performs a lane-switching action; otherwise, it remains in its current lane. After lane selection, the drone updates its speed based on either its preferred speed or the speed of its immediate predecessor in the selected lane, whichever is lower. The total cost consists of three components: position cost, speed cost, and collision cost. The position cost is designed to guide drones toward their target connection points and support lane switching decisions toward subsequent roads or ramps. The basic position cost is defined based on the hop distance between the current lane and the target lane associated with the next con-nection point, and the distance to that connection point. For example, consider a drone located at lane (0 , 0) with a target lane (1 , 0), assuming that all neighboring lanes are available and ac-cessible. The basic position cost for each candidate lane is defined as (example shown in Figure 1): 

• 0, for lane (1 , 0) 

• 1, for lanes (0 , 0), (0 , 1), and (1 , −1) 

• 2, for lanes ( −1, 0), (0 , −1), and ( −1, 1) If a drone does not have a target connection point (i.e., its destination is the current road /ramp.), its basic position cost is set to zero. The final position cost is further scaled by a multiplicative 8factor based on the distance between the drone’s current position and its target connection point. This factor is a monotonically non-negative decreasing function of distance, which reduces the influence of position cost for drones that are far from their targets, while increasing the switching priority for drones that are closer to their target connection points. The speed cost imposes a penalty on drones when their deployable speed is lower than their preferred speed. The deployable speed for each candidate lane is determined by the drone’s preferred speed, the speed of its predecessor, and the distance-dependent constraints. Here, the predecessor refers to the nearest ahead drone in the same lane. If the distance to the predecessor is below a predefined threshold and the predecessor’s speed is lower than the drone’s preferred speed, the deployable speed is set to the predecessor’s speed. Otherwise, it is set to the drone’s preferred speed. The speed cost is then computed using an increasing function of the gap between the preferred speed and the deployable speed. The collision cost is designed to prevent lane switching maneuvers that may lead to collisions. Its calculation consists of two steps. First, if any drone is detected in the candidate lane within a predefined distance of the current drone, the collision cost is set to the maximum value. This indicates that the candidate lane is infeasible for switching due to the high risk of an immediate collision. Second, if the cost is not set to the maximum value, the algorithm counts the number of drones in the lanes adjacent to the candidate lane that are within the same predefined distance of the current drone. The collision cost is then set equal to this count. This value represents the potential collision risk caused by multiple drones simultaneously attempting to switch into the same lane. A special rule is introduced for leading drones to break deadlocks that may occur when multiple drones are closely clustered and block one another. A leading drone is defined as the foremost drone that has no other drone ahead of it within a predefined safety distance. Such a drone is assigned the highest lane switching priority, and its cost in the second stage of the cost calculation is always set to zero. This allows the leading drone to switch lanes first, thereby creating opportunities for the following drones to resolve the blockage. The final cost is calculated as the weighted sum of the three cost components. The corre-sponding weights are configurable algorithm parameters that can be adjusted for di ff erent tra ffi cscenarios. After selecting the output lane and speed, the drone determines whether it is su ffi ciently close 9to its target connection point or the road closing point. If so, and the selected output lane is the current lane, the drone enters a stop state. This decision aims to help the drone avoid missing its target connection point or entering the closed section of the road. In this case, the interval before the next algorithm execution is reduced to allow the drone to leave the stop state more quickly when an opportunity becomes available. Otherwise, the interval is set to its normal value. The drone then maintains the selected speed and stays at, or switches to, the selected output lane until the next execution of the algorithm. 

3. System Model 

In this section, the scope of this study and the underlying assumptions are described. The sys-tem model is adopted from the previous STDG study [23] and is presented here for completeness before introducing the proposed prediction-based algorithm. 

3.1. Beacon Packet 

PB-STDG relies only on information exchanged through beacon packets. In STDG, the beacon mainly includes position and velocity information (several 3-D coordinates) of nearby drones. To better predict the decisions of other drones, the prediction-based algorithm extends the information included in each beacon while maintaining the same periodic beaconing rate. The newly introduced information types are: 

• Preferred speed : Each drone is assigned a preferred speed representing its optimal cruis-ing speed for maximum energy e ffi ciency based on its weight and physical design. This double value is included in the beacon to allow other drones to estimate the sender’s speed cost. 

• Lane being switched to : If the drone is currently switching lanes, it informs other drones of the lane it is switching to. Otherwise, this field remains empty. 

• Time for next algorithm execution : This information allows other drones to estimate the likelihood of the sender changing its movement decision in the near future. If a su ffi cient time gap exists before the next execution, other drones may assume that the sender will maintain its current movement state and will not participate in lane switching competition. 10 • Target lane : If the destination of a drone is located on a di ff erent road or ramp from its current position, the drone must switch to the lane containing the connection point leading toward that destination. Sharing this information enables other drones to estimate the sender’s position cost. Figure 2 shows the modified beacon structure and the corresponding length of each field. The original beacon size was 160 bytes, while the proposed extension increases it to 172 bytes. For the main content block, the original size was 116 bytes, and the newly introduced information adds only 12 bytes, extending the size to 128 bytes. 

> Figure 2: Beacon Content

An important assumption of this study is that each drone can accurately determine its current position and velocity using its onboard GPS without measurement uncertainty, and that all drones are time-synchronized via GPS. Assuming perfect GPS measurements allows any remaining in-formation uncertainty to be attributed to the communication process. Furthermore, the drone is assumed to be able to convert the GPS measurements, including latitude, longitude, altitude, speed, and heading, into the corresponding position and velocity representation used in the drone road system (DRS). In the DRS, position information consists of the road identifier, lane identi-fier, and the arc-length parameter along the current road segment. Velocity refers to the moving speed of the drone along the current road. 

3.2. Wireless Communication 

In the STDG system model, drones periodically broadcast beacon packets and obtain infor-mation about neighboring drones by receiving their beacons. A parameter, beaconing rate ( br ), is 11 used to represent the number of beacon transmissions per second (Hz). Then, the average beacon interval is approximately 1 /br , although a small amount of randomness is introduced into each interval to avoid synchronization e ff ects. In the prediction-based algorithm, a key modification to the beaconing mechanism is that a drone transmits an additional beacon immediately after completing an execution of the algorithm, in addition to the regularly scheduled beacon transmissions. This allows other drones to promptly obtain updated information about its movement state after a decision is made, providing more up-to-date information for predicting its subsequent actions. It is assumed that when an additional beacon is transmitted, the regular beacon timer is reset. Consequently, the next periodic beacon transmission will occur after another interval of approx-imately 1 /br seconds. Furthermore, beacon transmissions are assumed to be local broadcasts that do not require acknowledgments or routing protocols. Signal interference in the system is controlled by the beaconing rate and transmit power, and occurs only among beacon transmis-sions. Excluding external interference allows communication-related uncertainty to be attributed primarily to beacon collisions, packet loss, and transmission delays. Such interference may re-sult in beacon packet loss or transmission delay. This will be the main source of information uncertainty in the simulation. The beacon transmission mechanism follows the beaconing approach commonly adopted in IEEE 802.11p-based vehicular ad hoc networks (VANET) systems [13]. However, the physical-layer parameters are adapted for the drone communication scenario. In particular, a center fre-quency of 2.4 GHz, a bandwidth of 10 MHz, and a transmission bitrate of 12 Mbps are employed in the simulation. At the MAC layer, the IEEE 802.11 EDCA mechanism is adopted as the de-fault channel access scheme for vehicular ad hoc networks. In this work, only the best-e ff ort access category (AC_BE) is used. The contention window is configured with a minimum value of 31 and a maximum value of 1023, and the AIFS is set to 7. For the wireless channel model, a log-distance path loss model with a specified path loss exponent is adopted [28]. External interference, multipath fading, and shadowing e ff ects from buildings or other structures are not considered in this work. 12 4. Proposed PB-STDG Algorithm 

The proposed algorithm is a variant of the previous short-term decentralized greedy (STDG) algorithm [23], which relies on additional shared information in the beaconing packet to reduce the upcoming decision uncertainty of neighboring drones. This algorithm requires drones to operate within the drone road system formalism proposed in [22]. 

4.1. Preliminaries 

One challenge of STDG is the uncertainty of neighboring drones. With limited shared infor-mation, it is hard to predict the upcoming actions of neighbors. Thus, to avoid most potential risks, drones should choose conservative decisions, such as slowing down or keeping the current lane, which lowers the average speed and throughput. In addition, under these actions drones tend to stay in outer lanes which have fewer neighboring lanes. Based on the preliminary study (tested DRS is shown in Figure 10), the outer lane usage ( ≈ 22%) is noticeably higher than the inner lane usage ( ≈ 13%). This leads to low utilization of inner lanes and reduced e ffi ciency of the DRS. Based on the observations, achieving the lowest collision rate ( <0.01%) results in an average drone speed of only 7.06 m /s, well below the preferred cruising speed of 12.5 m /s. When the algorithm is configured to disregard most potential risks from neighboring drones, the average speed increases to 11.6 m /s, while the collision rate increases only slightly to 0.36%. When all potential risks are disregarded, the average speed further increases to 12.3 m /s, with a collision rate of 1.27%. These results indicate that maintaining a close-to-zero collision rate requires drones to substantially reduce their speed to account for potential collisions that may not actually occur, such as when neighboring drones continue along their current trajectories. This is the motivation of the proposed prediction-based guidance algorithm. It aims to make safer and more e ffi cient decisions that improve average speed, throughput, and DRS utilization while maintaining the close-to-zero collision rate achieved by the coordination-free STDG ap-proach. We assume that all drones run the same guidance algorithm and know their end-to-end paths, which can be planned by other algorithms, and are also familiar with the entire DRS within which they operate. 13 At the highest level, the algorithm first filters competitor drones from all nearby drones and predicts a decision for each according to their priority which is mainly determined by its current position. The drones are considered competitors when they would run the algorithm on their side within a short time. After each predicted decision, the algorithm assumes the relevant competitor will make the same decision and then simulates its e ff ect on the current environment and updates relevant parameters for the prediction. This process is repeated until no competitor has higher priority than the current drone. The algorithm then makes the actual decision for the current drone based on iteratively updated parameters for the current drone, specifying the speed and lane to be used until the next invocation. 4.2. The Motivation of Splitting Blocks 

In prediction-based scenarios, drones are assumed to exchange additional information, which improves the accuracy of predicting other drones’ actions. By knowing how others make their decisions, it becomes easier to plan a safe and e ffi cient path for the current drone. However, due to various limitations such as communication range and packet loss, drones cannot obtain complete global information about all drones in the system. Moreover, even neighboring drones 14 may possess di ff erent information. In such cases, predictions may not match the actual actions when the predictor’s knowledge di ff ers from that of the predicted drones. To improve prediction accuracy, we divide each road into multiple equal-length blocks whose boundaries are normal to the central lane. With a known block length ϵb and the distance trav-eled along the road Lt, a drone can determine the block to which it currently belongs as ⌊Lt/ϵ b⌋.Drones only predict the actions of others within the same block, while treating drones in other blocks as potential risks. The process is shown in Figure 4. Drones D and E belong to the same block, and E has higher priority since it is ahead. When D runs the algorithm, it first simulates E and then makes its own decision based on the simulation result. When E runs the algorithm, it directly makes its own decision due to its highest priority without considering D. During the decision-making and prediction process, drones in other blocks, such as F, are considered unpre-dictable, and D and E use the previous coordination-free approach to account for them. 

> Figure 4: Road division into equal-length blocks for more accurate prediction

In the above process, drone D knows how E will make its decision and already has all relevant information, so this prediction has a higher probability of being correct. In contrast, without using blocks, a drone only predicts the actions of nearby drones within a certain range. For example, D may predict E but not F, while E predicts F. In this case, D knows that E will predict F, and F will also predict some other drones further ahead. Since D does not have complete information about all drones ahead, it cannot make an accurate prediction of E’s actions. 15 Without using blocks, even if the prediction accuracy is su ffi ciently high, another challenge arises from the dependency chain introduced by the prediction process. Each drone’s decision depends on the predicted decisions of nearby drones, particularly those ahead of it. Consequently, on a long road with high drone density, the rearmost drone must first predict the decision of the frontmost drone, followed by the second frontmost drone, and so on, before it can determine its own action. As the number of drones increases, this dependency chain substantially increases the computational complexity and results in many unnecessary calculations for drones that are too far away to have any meaningful influence on the current drone’s decision. The use of blocks reduces the computational complexity and breaks the chain of dependence: drones only simulate neighbors within the same block and treat all other drones as conservatively as in the coordination-free approach. In addition, within one block it is easy to establish a priority system based on position and MAC address (to break the tie). Drones near the front boundary of a block (such as E) will make keep-current-lane decisions similar to the previous method. However, drones behind them (like D) in the same block can correctly simulate these decisions and determine that they have higher priority than other competitors, and that the current situation is safe enough to start lane switching. Drones further behind D will also move into the inner lane if the available gap is su ffi cient and they win the priority. In this way, road usage is distributed more evenly across the lanes. 

4.3. Selection of Block Length 

In the proposed algorithm, a block is introduced to coordinate lane-switching decisions in crowded tra ffi c conditions, where drones cannot determine whether neighboring drones are also attempting to switch to the same lane. Drones within the same block predict other drones’ de-cisions and determine their own actions accordingly. The block length is therefore a critical parameter, as it directly a ff ects the computational complexity, e ffi ciency, and prediction accuracy of the algorithm. Let n denote the number of neighboring drones detected by drone A. In the original STDG algorithm, the computational complexity is O(n), as drone A evaluates each neighboring drone once when calculating the cost function and making the decision. In PB-STDG, drone A may need to predict the decisions of multiple neighboring drones within the same block before making its own decision. The computational complexity then increases to O(mn ), where m is the number 16 of neighboring drones located within the same block. Since m is directly determined by the block length, increasing the block length also increases the computational complexity of the algorithm. Within each block, drones closer to the front boundary are given higher priority when mak-ing decisions. However, these drones may need to consider neighboring drones located in the next-ahead block, whose future actions cannot be predicted. Hence, these neighboring drones are treated as unpredictable potential collision risks. With the additional shared information, includ-ing the target lane and the scheduled time of the next algorithm execution, drones can identify and filter out some of these potential collision risks, thereby reducing unnecessary conservatism during the decision-making process. But to ensure safety, the drones at the front of a block may still adopt conservative actions 1 similar to those in the original STDG algorithm, often slowing down or stopping unnecessarily. The overall e ffi ciency of the algorithm is therefore influenced by the proportion of drones a ff ected by this e ff ect. Increasing the block length decreases the number of blocks and therefore reduces the proportion of drones that may make conservative decisions because of neighboring drones in next-ahead blocks. In the previous STDG and the proposed algorithm, ϵ1 defines the distance threshold used to determine whether a neighboring drone is considered a direct or potential collision risk. If another drone is within ϵ1 of the current drone, it is treated as a direct collision risk if it is in the candidate lane 2. If it is in a lane adjacent to the candidate lane, it is instead treated as a potential collision risk. A block length satisfying ϵb < ϵ 1 may cause neighboring drones within the potential collision range to be excluded from the prediction process. This is more likely to occur under high-density tra ffi c conditions since drones are closely spaced. Therefore, the prediction-based algorithm may make overly aggressive decisions and lead to an increased collision rate. To ensure that all relevant neighboring drones are considered during prediction, the block length should satisfy 

ϵb ≥ ϵ1.

The final factor influenced by the block length is the prediction accuracy. In the proposed algorithm, drones predict the decisions of neighboring drones based on the information learned

To achieve accurate predictions, the information available to the predicting drone should be as consistent as possible with the actual information available to the predicted drone. The worst-case communication requirement occurs when the rearmost drone in the current block needs to predict the frontmost drone in the same block. To ensure that the rearmost drone can receive the same safety information as the frontmost drone, the distance can be as large as twice the block length. ϵb < communication range 

2Combining the above considerations, the block length should be carefully selected. It should not be too large, as this increases the computational complexity and reduces prediction accuracy, nor should it be too small, as this may cause the prediction process to ignore nearby drones. Based on this trade-o ff , the default block length used in this paper is set to 50 m, which is larger than the appropriate value of 15 m for ϵ1 identified in the previous study and smaller than half of the communication range ( ≈ 120 m). The performance of other block lengths is also evaluated in the simulations to validate this parameter selection. Based on the assumption that the block length ϵb is set to an appropriate value, we restrict the prediction-based algorithm to consider only drones in the current block and adjacent blocks. This restriction reduces the computational complexity while retaining the information most relevant to short-term collision avoidance and prediction.

• Drones are assumed to have knowledge of the tested DRS. Once a drone is injected into the system, it is assigned a random destination. The end-to-end path from its start point to the destination is also known by the drone, and it will follow that path during its whole lifespan. 27 • Drones in the road system are treated as points. A collision is considered to occur when the distance between two drones is smaller than the minimum safety distance. The two drones involved in the collision are immediately removed from the simulation and no longer a ff ect other drones in the system. However, their safety information is still retained by other drones until no packet has been received from them for ten seconds. 

• For each lane with an entry point at the beginning of the road, drones are continuously injected into the lane at randomly determined time intervals. The inter-arrival time is drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean determined by the Generation Rate .The generation rate represents the ideal number of drones generated per second for each lane. However, when the road system becomes fully occupied by drones, additional drones cannot be injected. In such a case, the actual number of drones injected into the system per second per lane is referred to as the In jection Rate , which can be much lower than the 

Generation Rate under high-density conditions. 

• Once a drone reaches its destination, it is immediately removed from the simulation, but its information remains in other drones’ neighbor tables until the corresponding entries expire. The neighbor table stores all safety information received from other drones, and its entries are maintained as soft state. 

• Drones accurately know and send their current position to neighbors. GPS noise is ignored during the simulation. 

• In the original STDG algorithm, a threshold of 2 ϵ1 is used to identify potential risks in neighboring lanes, whereas the prediction-based algorithm uses a threshold of ϵ1. To pro-vide a consistent basis for comparison, a threshold of ϵ1 is used for both algorithms in all subsequent simulations. This adjustment increases the average speed of STDG, while its collision rate remains close to that obtained with the original threshold. The key performance metrics are Collision Rate (cr) , which is the fraction of collided drones among all injected drones; Average Speed (as) , which indicates the average speed of all injected drones; and Throughput , which is the number of drones arriving at their destinations per second, also referred to as the arrival count. With low collision rates, the throughput should be practically the same as the injection rate. The preliminary study shows that the system reaches a steady state 28 after an initial transient period. The detailed period depends on the road system, and the largest value among all tested road systems is 200 seconds. Thus we only count the throughput after that time in all simulations. Additional metrics will also be investigated for specific purposes in the following sections. For all following simulations, we run 50 replications and the presented results are the average values for these replications to ensure they are statistically meaningful.

5.6. Discussion 

In the simulation study, we investigate both the previous STDG algorithm and the proposed prediction-based algorithm under various parameter settings across several road systems. Across all simulation results, a common conclusion is that the prediction-based algorithm consistently 39 achieves higher average speeds while maintaining comparable or lower collision rates compared to the STDG algorithm in all tested scenarios. This advantage becomes more pronounced when the drone density is high or when a large proportion of nearby drones need to switch lanes frequently. For example, in the complex drone road system scenario, the average speed increases from 10.28 m /s to 12.18 m /s when using the prediction-based algorithm. In the scenario shown in Figure 7, the prediction-based algorithm provides at least a 35% improvement in average speed without degrading the collision rate. The additional prediction mechanism provides a possible explanation for these observations. During the previous study, the challenge of STDG in the feasible-lane selection phase was that drones could not determine whether nearby drones also intended to switch to the same lane. Un-der the low-collision-rate strategy (i.e., low position cost weight and high collision cost weight), drones tend to make highly conservative decisions. A candidate lane can be easily discarded when nearby drones are detected in its neighboring lanes, causing the stay-in-current-lane be-havior to become the preferred option. This consequently reduces the overall average speed. Under the speed-oriented strategy (i.e., high position-cost weight and low collision-cost weight), the issue described above can be alleviated. However, the trade-o ff is that drones tend to ignore the risk of concurrent switching, which consequently leads to a higher collision risk. PB-STDG is able to balance these two strategies. Since drones share additional information with neighboring drones, such as target lanes and preferred speeds, they can predict the decisions of nearby drones and make more informed decisions accordingly. In the evaluated scenario, only a small number of drones near the front boundary of a block need to explicitly consider concurrent switching conflicts. Most drones instead rely on predicted behaviors of neighboring drones and select either the optimal lane or a secondary optimal lane if the preferred lane is already occupied. Drones then have a greater opportunity to switch to lanes that either allow higher cruising speeds or lead toward their target destinations without unnecessary slowing or stopping. To further validate this finding, we collect the total number of algorithm executions and the number of decisions that switch lanes for both algorithms in the same four-road, seven-ramp DRS with the same parameter configuration (10-10000-15 as κ1-κ2-ϵ1, and gr = 0.2). The results are shown in Table 7. From the results, the prediction-based algorithm performs fewer algorithm executions but 40 Algorithm Total Algorithm Executions Lane-Switching Decisions

Both cases help the drone reach its destination faster and consequently improve the throughput. Therefore, the substantial increase in lane-switching decisions made by the prediction-based algorithm in-dicates that it can encourage drones to safely perform lane switching, thereby improving tra ffi ceffi ciency. In addition, since the number of drones is similar for both algorithms and drones execute the algorithm more frequently when they are in the stop state, fewer algorithm executions suggest that drones using the prediction-based algorithm are less likely to remain in the stop state. This indirectly demonstrates the higher operational e ffi ciency of drones under the prediction-based algorithm. Based on the additional simulation results on lane usage distribution, the previously proposed inner lane vanishing hypothesis is not an accurate explanation of the limitation of the STDG algorithm. The results suggest that, without any lane-switching demand, the STDG algorithm does not noticeably a ff ect the lane usage distribution and therefore does not cause inner lanes to vanish. However, when a large proportion of drones share the same target lane, the STDG algorithm can cause the target lane to gradually fall out of use. This occurs because many drones fail to switch to the desired lane in order to avoid potential collisions with other drones attempting the same maneuver. As a result, these drones may accumulate in the surrounding lanes and block the target lane. Figure 9 indicates that the PB-STDG algorithm can mitigate the lane vanishing problem by applying the same mechanism for resolving concurrent switching conflicts discussed above. In addition, it leads the lane usage distribution toward a more even pattern, but high-demand lanes still have correspondingly high usage. In contrast, under the STDG algorithm, some high-demand lanes remain underutilized. This is one of the main reasons why the prediction-based 41 algorithm achieves a higher average speed. However, in an extremely crowded scenario, where a large number of drones have the same target ramp and become congested before entering it, the prediction-based algorithm provides limited improvement in the throughput from the road to the target ramp. In this case, the bottle-neck is no longer caused by the decision-making process but by the limited capacity of the drone road system, which is determined by drone density and road system design. Based on the results of varying the block length ϵb, ϵb should not be set to a small value, especially when it is smaller than ϵ1. This is because drones only consider neighbors belonging to the same or adjacent blocks as potential collision risks. When ϵb is small, drones only consider a relatively limited area (their current and adjacent blocks) around themselves and may ignore other drones that are physically close but located outside the considered blocks. In this case, the collision rate increases noticeably. When the drone density is high, a larger ϵb value may slightly increase the collision rate. This is because a larger block size means that more neighboring drones need to be considered during the prediction process. An incorrect prediction for any neighboring drone may a ff ect the decision-making process and eventually lead to a collision. Therefore, an appropriate value of ϵb should be larger than the threshold ϵ1 but should not be excessively large, as it may increase the prediction complexity and prediction error rate. The sensitivity study indicates that the environmental parameter generation rate has the great-est influence on average speed and throughput, while the algorithm parameter ϵ1 has the most significant e ff ect on collision rate. In comparison, the remaining parameters, κ1 and κ2, have only limited influence on the performance metrics in the prediction-based algorithm. Under the STDG algorithm, however, κ1 and κ2 contribute more substantially to system performance. This phenomenon is expected for both algorithms. In the STDG algorithm, the κ1/κ 2 ratio controls the strategy used to handle concurrent switching problems. A higher ratio leads to more aggressive decisions, whereas a relatively lower ratio results in more conservative behavior. These di ff erent strategies then produce variations in both collision rate and average speed. In the prediction-based algorithm, κ2 is often less influential because the movements of nearby drones can be predicted, making it unnecessary to heavily consider potential collision risks in most cases. As a result, varying κ1 alone does not substantially alter the candidate lane pool. Consequently, the average speeds across di ff erent parameter settings remain close to each other. 42 Although the prediction-based algorithm performs better than the STDG algorithm in our evaluated scenarios, it still has several disadvantages that are not directly reflected in the simula-tion results. The first issue is the increased computational complexity. For STDG, the computa-tional complexity depends on the number of nearby drones and is a ff ected by the transmit power. Under the same transmit power, since drones are required to predict the movements of nearby drones within the same block, the overall complexity increases from O(n) to O(mn ), where n is the number of nearby drones and m is the number of nearby drones within the same block. In addition, a relatively minor issue is the vulnerability introduced by the additional information shared in beacon packets. Such information may be manipulated to gain higher lane switching priority. For example, a drone could falsely advertise a higher preferred speed, thereby increas-ing the speed cost calculated by neighboring drones and causing them to avoid a lane that the malicious drone subsequently switches into. 

6. Conclusion and Future Work 

This paper presents PB-STDG, a prediction-based extension of the Short-Term Decentral-ized Greedy (STDG) guidance algorithm. The proposed algorithm inherits the cost-function mechanism of STDG, which encourages drones to continuously make short-term greedy deci-sions to improve tra ffi c e ffi ciency. It further introduces a prediction mechanism that enables drones to anticipate the decisions of neighboring drones using the additional information shared in beacon packets. This prediction process alleviates the over-conservative behavior observed in high-density tra ffi c scenarios. By predicting the intended movements of neighboring drones, each drone can make more informed decisions. A series of simulation studies was conducted to evaluate the proposed prediction-based algo-rithm and compare its performance with the original STDG algorithm under the same drone road system. We first investigated the e ff ects of wireless communication parameters, including the beaconing rate and transmit power. The results show that, within a reasonable range, these pa-rameters have little impact on the algorithm performance. We then compared the two algorithms under di ff erent drone road system configurations and algorithm parameter settings. The simula-tion results demonstrate that the proposed PB-STDG consistently achieves higher average speed and throughput while maintaining the same or even lower collision rate in almost all scenarios. 43 The sensitivity analysis and the block length study further show that the influence of the orig-inal cost-function weights, κ1 and κ2, is noticeably reduced in the prediction-based algorithm. Instead, the performance is mainly determined by the distance threshold ϵ1 and the newly intro-duced block length parameter ϵb. With appropriate parameter settings, the algorithm achieves an average speed close to the ideal cruising speed while maintaining a close-to-zero collision rate. The main concern for the prediction-based algorithm is its computational complexity, which depends on the number of drones within the same block. Choosing a smaller value of ϵb can reduce the additional computational cost, but it may also increase the collision rate because the prediction process may fail to include all nearby drones. Another potential issue is the vulner-ability introduced by the additional shared information. Malicious drones may transmit false information to gain priority during lane-switching competition. Finally, this study assumes ideal operating conditions by neglecting GPS positioning errors, interference from other wireless sig-nals, environmental noise, and ionospheric or tropospheric disturbances. Therefore, the simula-tion results may not fully represent real-world scenarios. Future research may address these limitations in several directions. One possible approach is to develop a purely coordination-based or negotiation-based guidance algorithm that avoids complex prediction and cost calculations. Alternatively, the prediction mechanism could be fur-ther optimized to reduce computational complexity while incorporating data validation or trust mechanisms to improve the reliability of the shared information. In addition, more realistic com-munication and positioning models should be incorporated into the simulation to evaluate the robustness and applicability of the proposed algorithm under practical operating conditions. To further improve the realism of the simulation, developing more accurate models of drone dynam-ics and the lane-switching process is another possible direction.
