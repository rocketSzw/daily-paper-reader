# Borrowed Eyes: Markerless Nano-UAV Flight with an Active Quadruped Observer 

Alejandro Lorite Mora 1, Dimitrios Arapis 2 and Andr´ es Fa´ ı˜ na 1

Abstract — Nano unmanned aerial vehicles (nano-UAVs) can navigate confined spaces that larger robots cannot, but their payload capacity severely limits the sensors and compute avail-able for self-localization in global navigation satellite system (GNSS)-denied environments. We present a vision-based system that localizes a nano-UAV from a quadruped robot with an arm-mounted camera. The quadruped tracks the drone, estimates its position in its own coordinate system using segmentation masks and depth, and transmits that position over a real-time radio link. To ensure continuous tracking, we developed a perception-aware nonlinear Model Predictive Controller (NMPC) that dy-namically adjusts the quadruped’s body and arm to maximize the drone’s visibility, treating observation reliability as a pri-mary control objective. Relying solely on this external estimate, the nano-UAV executed predefined trajectories from takeoff to landing with a median 3D localization error of 59 mm. The system demonstrates robustness against short visual occlusions by smoothly transitioning to onboard inertial flight when line-of-sight is temporarily lost. Ultimately, this framework allows a quadruped to offload the localization burden of a nano-UAV, enabling inspection of complex spaces that neither robot could navigate alone. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Industrial assets such as storage tanks, pressure vessels, pipes, and tunnels require regular inspection for corrosion, cracks, and other structural damage. Manual inspection is time-consuming, costly, and hazardous, especially in narrow or elevated spaces. Nano unmanned aerial vehicles (nano-UAVs), weighing only tens of grams, are well suited to reaching and inspecting areas inaccessible to larger robots. However, autonomous nano-UAV deployment is severely constrained by onboard localization. Larger UAVs can carry stereo cameras or LiDAR to run visual-inertial odometry (VIO) or simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), but these sensors exceed the payload and power capacity of nano-scale platforms [1]. Confined industrial settings also feature repetitive, textureless steel surfaces and uneven illumination, which degrade feature-based localization even when sufficient computation is available. Rather than increasing the sensing burden on the drone or instrumenting the environment, we offload localization to a mobile ground robot with greater sensing and com-putational capacity (Fig. 1). The quadruped localizes itself and estimates the 3D position of the nano-UAV using an arm-mounted camera, while the drone flies using this exter-nally supplied estimate. Crucially, the estimate is expressed directly in the quadruped’s global coordinate map rather than in an onboard frame. This provides a shared spatial representation for heterogeneous collaboration, allowing the nano-UAV to scout confined spaces and report points of interest in a frame the ground robot can directly use. Our prior work [2] demonstrated this cooperative division of labor using fiducial markers on a nano-UAV [3]. However, physical markers add considerable payload mass, increase aerodynamic drag, and enlarge the vehicle’s footprint. In addition, reactive camera tracking often loses visual contact during fast maneuvers or temporary occlusions. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a mark-erless localization and active observation framework for nano-UAVs. Our main contributions are: (i) Markerless external localization: A position-only perception pipeline that estimates the 3D position of a bare nano-UAV in the quadruped’s map frame, using depth-integrity gates to arXiv:2610.09967v1 [cs.RO] 7 Oct 2026 discard corrupted measurements and convert sensor failures into safe dropouts. (ii) Perception-aware active observa-tion: A nonlinear Model Predictive Controller (NMPC) that coordinates the quadruped’s body and arm motion to preserve line-of-sight and optimize viewing geometry. (iii) Closed-loop flight under intermittent perception: Closed-loop flight validation from takeoff to landing on the external vision feed alone, demonstrating autonomous recovery from visual occlusions through onboard inertial coasting. II. R ELATED WORK 

Ground-Aerial Collaborative Localization. Leveraging ground robots to assist UAV localization is an established strategy for global navigation satellite system (GNSS)-denied environments. Existing approaches utilize ultra-wideband radios on both platforms to achieve decimeter-level local-ization [4], employ ground vehicles as external references to refine onboard state estimates [5], or use legged robots as mobile magnetic beacons, achieving tracking errors of 8 to 11 cm [6]. These methods either require dedicated onboard payload hardware [5] or provide lower accuracy than close-range visual localization [4], [6]. Markerless inter-robot observation has been explored in multi-robot SLAM, where mutually observing robots use visual detections to refine their state estimates [7]. However, such observations have not been demonstrated as the primary external position source for closed-loop flight of a nano-UAV. Removing fiducial markers eliminates added mass and aerodynamic penalties, but requires localizing a small, dark, weakly textured, and symmetric airframe directly from appearance and depth. While render-and-compare 6D pose estimators [8], [9] address similar challenges, we exploit the flight controller’s internal attitude estimation and compute only 3D position, reducing the vision pipeline to the essential external state (Section III-B). 

Vision-Based Localization of Small Aerial Targets. 

Prior work on aerial target detection predominantly targets larger platforms. Vrba et al. [10] detected a 0.55 m quadrotor using stereo vision up to 50 m, with subsequent work report-ing ranges up to 20 m [11]. Carrio et al. [12] localized drones using onboard depth maps at 16 Hz up to 8 m, but evaluated a 517 mm platform and attributed range limits to sensor constraints rather than target dimensions. In LiDAR-based studies [13], classification degrades when a target returns fewer than 40 points. A bare nano-UAV challenges these limits at much closer range, where its small surface area returns very few depth points. We explicitly quantify this relationship up to 3.1 m in Section IV-A. 

Aerial Navigation Through Narrow Apertures. Navigat-ing confined spaces has been addressed through collision-tolerant platforms operating through manhole-sized open-ings [14], [15], or commercial caged inspection drones with full onboard SLAM. 1 Both strategies require structural protection and compute payloads well beyond the capacity of nano-UAVs. A. System overview 

The proposed system is shown in Fig. 1 and its processing pipeline in Fig. 2. A Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped serves as the mobile base, equipped with its own arm-mounted gripper camera, which provides color and registered time-of-flight depth (Section III-C), and an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin onboard computer. This computer handles quadruped control, arm articulation, and drone pose estimation. From each RGB-D frame, the pipeline extracts at most one 3D position of the drone, referenced to the quadruped’s map frame. Validated position estimates are transmitted via a low-latency radio link to the drone (a Bitcraze Crazyflie relying solely on an inertial measurement unit (IMU)) using the 

crazyswarm2 server. 2

The quadruped tracks its motion via onboard odometry, anchored to the world map frame by a static transform (Tmap →odom in Eq. (1)). In these experiments, an OptiTrack motion-capture system supplies that anchor and records ground truth for calibration and evaluation. Motion capture never provides state estimates to the flight controller: the drone completes its entire flight profile, from takeoff to landing, relying on the markerless pipeline. Anchoring in-stead to the quadruped’s onboard SLAM map adds negligible error: Spot’s internal map drifted < 1 cm (P95). We use the motion-capture anchor to keep reported tracking performance independent of ground-robot self-localization error.

2) Auto-Labeled Training Data: To bypass manual an-notation, we trained the segmentation model on in-domain imagery from an automated pipeline. We prompted a foun-dation model (SAM 2 [16]) using projected motion-capture positions of the drone. Motion capture is used solely as a prompting mechanism for the labeling pipeline: the live detector never sees external motion-capture data, ensuring no circularity in training. The training set holds 7 306 positive frames: 6 025 auto-labeled from nine recordings of the drone hanging and in flight, and 1 281 composites that paste segmented drones onto footage of the surrounding rooms recorded without the drone, so the detector meets the route’s backgrounds before it flies there. A further 1 099 drone-free frames supply negatives, and two recordings serve as test sets. Seven retraining runs as the dataset expanded required 

4.2 GPU-hours across laptop and desktop GPUs, with the deployed model fine-tuned in 0.31 h with zero manual labels.

By construction, the drone is the nearest surface inside a correct mask. C. Camera, Calibration, and Ground Truth 

The gripper camera’s pose is intrinsically derived from the quadruped’s kinematic chain. Its time-of-flight depth successfully returns ranges on the dark airframe where a stereo camera (e.g., Intel RealSense D435) fails. Accuracy metrics compare the estimated drone position against motion-capture ground truth. For the gripper camera, the tracking chain relies on the quadruped’s kinematics registered to the motion-capture frame. We report raw accuracy through the calibrated chain alongside precision after rigid Horn/Markley alignment of the estimated and reference tracks, which ab-sorbs a constant offset in the reference chain and so isolates the estimator’s intrinsic precision. Experiments evaluate three configurations: the deployed bare airframe, an airframe with a ∼1.8 g matte panel restoring stereo depth (diagnostic), and an airframe equipped with an AprilTag (fiducial baseline). 

D. Single-Estimator Fusion, IMU Coasting, and Latency 

The drone operates an extended Kalman filter (EKF) that fuses the markerless position updates with high-rate attitude and yaw estimates from its onboard IMU (Bosch BMI088). The drone maintains no map frame of its own. Between external position updates, the filter integrates IMU measure-ments to coast, with uncertainty swelling during coasting and shrinking upon correction. Visual processing and radio transmission introduce a pipeline latency of ∼180 ms, so each estimate describes where the drone was, not where it is. The flights reported here fuse every estimate on arrival, without latency compensation, which leaves a position lag along the velocity vector. The measurement noise of each estimate is set to 5 cm, matching the measured error of the markerless pipeline, rather than to the millimeter trust used for motion capture. The residual lag is one source of the tracking error in Section IV-B. 

E. Perception-Aware Active Observation 

To maintain continuous localization, the quadruped must actively keep the drone near the camera’s optical center and within its reliable depth range. A naive controller aiming at the last known position lags behind fast maneuvers, causing the drone to exit the field of view. Instead, we formulate observation quality as the primary objective of a nonlin-ear model predictive controller (NMPC), commanding the quadruped’s body and arm to anticipate the drone’s motion using its known trajectory. F. Position-only control and the rate floor 

Two preliminary results set the estimator’s design targets. First, we send the drone only its position, and its EKF takes attitude and yaw from the IMU, as ultra-wideband and Lighthouse positioning systems do [4]. Flown on motion capture, this position-only mode matched full-pose control on every paired trajectory. Only yaw is given up: it drifts 9 to 10 ◦ per minute, against 0.8◦ under full-pose control, a bounded wander rather than a divergence. Second, we measured what the flight demands of the position feed. A shim degrades the motion-capture position one axis at a time, in update rate, latency, noise, and dropout, and the drone flies on the degraded feed. Only the rate axis resolved a clean crossing in this single-pass session: hover remained stable down to 5 Hz and broke down at 1 Hz, so the markerless pipeline must publish above 5 Hz, and Section IV-A shows that it does, at about four times that rate. The latency, noise, and dropout axes remain preliminary. 

Code and data. All the software described in this paper, comprising the position estimator, the labeling tools, the drone firmware and the changes to crazyswarm2, together with the recorded trials, will be published on acceptance. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

Three experimental campaigns evaluate the system against our contributions: (A) markerless localization accuracy and depth integrity gating, (B) active observation and predictive tracking, and (C) closed-loop flight with no external local-ization in the flight loop, including visual occlusions and envelope limits. Together, these experiments demonstrate that the quadruped tracks the bare nano-UAV, maintains visibility, and closes the flight control loop on visual estimates alone. 

A. Markerless localization accuracy and integrity gating 

We evaluate localization accuracy against motion-capture ground truth under two regimes. Offline, on hovering flight recordings with a stationary arm ( 1.6 to 1.8 m distance), we evaluate static estimation accuracy across 205 ground-truth synchronized frames. Live, we evaluate the system across eight closed-loop flights ( 1 069 s total airborne time) with an actively moving camera spanning 0.6 to 2.3 m stand-off, measuring accuracy under real operational conditions. 

Accuracy and coverage. On the hovering recording, the detector identifies the drone in 89% of frames, yielding a median 3D error of 38 .6 mm ( 32 .8 mm lateral). In live flight (Fig. 3), the median 3D error across 17 363 positions is 49 mm (P95 119 mm), with lateral and along-axis com-ponents contributing equally ( 31 mm median each). Only 

0.6% of positions deviate by more than 300 mm from truth. Live errors are slightly higher than static figures because the camera moves dynamically. To isolate camera motion from drone motion, we conducted trials tracking a hovering drone: over a shared distance band, a stationary camera achieves 43 mm median error versus 55 to 58 mm when tracking, showing that camera motion incurs only a modest accuracy penalty. The pipeline publishes updates at 20 Hz whenever the drone is visible, comfortably exceeding the 

5 Hz flight stability threshold. In an unmapped room absent from training data, background clutter caused false detections in 3.3% of frames at a 0.35 confidence threshold ( 10 .7% at 

0.25 ), which the integrity gates filter out.

Raw depth returns on the bare airframe yield a median of zero valid depth pixels out to 3.1 m. on the bare airframe to 80 .5% with the panel, causing motor saturation and emergency landing after 23 s. Gating protects against missing depth without adding airborne mass.

D. Loss of Line of Sight and Flown Envelope 

Ten additional flights stress-tested the pipeline against visual occlusions and kinematic limits: a person crossing between the robots, a step ladder in the near field, and flights near the arm’s elevation limit. During dropouts, the bridge sent no position updates, forcing the drone to coast on its IMU under two hierarchical timers. The first is an experimental safety switch that returns control to motion capture if no visual estimate arrives for 4.0 s. The second is an onboard failsafe watchdog that commands an emergency landing if all position streams cease for 4.5 s.

D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

This work demonstrates that a quadruped robot can provide a closed-loop, markerless position stream accu-rate enough to pilot a nano-UAV, eliminating the need for payload-intensive onboard fiducials or external motion-capture systems. By combining a segmentation mask with registered depth and enforcing depth-integrity gating, the pipeline localized a bare nano-UAV using the quadruped’s gripper camera with a 3D median error of 49 mm over 1 069 sof closed-loop flight. Notably, the underlying detector was trained entirely on automatically generated masks, requiring zero manual labeling. Relying exclusively on this markerless stream, the drone executed extended 120 s hovers and au-tonomous ten-waypoint trajectories from takeoff to landing with a 58 to 60 mm median localization error against motion capture. Concurrently, the quadruped used perception-aware NMPC to actively maintain the drone within the camera’s field of view, while the drone’s IMU safely coasted through transient line-of-sight occlusions lasting up to 2.8 s. The primary generalizable insight of this work is the emphasis on outlier rejection over naive estimate smoothing. On lightweight airframes, depth-channel failures are frequent and severe. Our architecture demonstrates that converting spurious readings into dropouts is substantially safer than passing corrupted positions to the drone: an onboard IMU filter readily coasts through brief dropouts, whereas a con-fidently false position estimate destabilizes the flight con-troller. Evaluated on the stereo camera setup, these gates eliminated all meter-scale background locks across 5 539 test frames. A secondary insight is the value of establishing an empirical performance budget: our baseline confirmed that stable flight requires at least a 5 Hz update rate, which our pipeline comfortably exceeded by providing 20 Hz updates whenever the drone remained in view. A central practical insight is the benefit of exploiting the quadruped’s integrated hand camera rather than adding an external sensor payload. Commercial quadruped arms and grippers already package high-resolution optical and time-of-flight depth sensors accompanied by factory kinematic cali-bration. Repurposing these built-in sensors avoids mounting extra cameras, custom brackets, or auxiliary wiring harnesses on the quadruped, while the drone flies at its stock 37 gwith no added payload. This demonstrates that collaborative heterogeneous localization requires no specialized sensory retrofitting when platforms share existing perception. Several limitations bound our results. First, the detector was trained on laboratory recordings with uniform back-grounds, confining experiments to that environment. Rotating the camera introduced novel background clutter absent from training, requiring the quadruped to sidestep to maintain background consistency (Section IV-C). The drone’s small, dark airframe returns sparse time-of-flight depth points, making depth extraction sensitive to surface orientation and ambient illumination. Second, the operational envelope is limited by the kinematic coupling between camera pitch and standoff distance. To maintain a stationary background for the detector, the quadruped keeps its camera level and tracks the drone primarily by adjusting arm height and standoff distance rather than pitching upward. Observing a higher drone forces the quadruped to increase standoff distance, pushing the target beyond the 2.3 m maximum effective range of the time-of-flight depth sensor. Tracking remains reliable between ranges of 0.6 to 2.3 m and up to 1.65 m flight height, and degrades at 2.0 m (Section IV-D). We expect a ceiling near 3.5 m by relaxing wrist pitch penalties for steeper camera tilt, though our laboratory ceiling bounded validation. Third, the architecture requires direct line-of-sight: once the drone navigates deep into cluttered structures, a single viewpoint loses observability [17]. Finally, flight campaigns spanning three hovers, three full-loop autonomous flights, ten occlusion and clutter probe flights, twelve controller flights, and nine reference flights demonstrate practical viability rather than exhaustive statistical certifications. Looking ahead, future work will focus on expanding robustness and generalizability toward real-world industrial deployments. First, visual limitations can be mitigated by training on diverse datasets with extensive augmentation, paired with airframe modifications such as high-contrast col-oring and small matte surfaces for reliable depth extraction. Second, we plan to integrate cooperative path planning that coordinates joint robot trajectories over extended inspection missions. Finally, to overcome single-viewpoint observability limits in cluttered structures, future architectures could in-corporate complementary modalities such as radio-frequency ranging, optical beacons, or onboard relative tracking.
