ABSTRACT
Recent vision-and-language navigation (VLN) systems increasingly adopt stream-
ing Video-LLM policies that map egocentric RGB observations and instructions
directly to low-level actions. Yet these policies inherit weak 3D geometric pri-
ors from 2D pretraining. Existing geometry-aware extensions charge a persistent
inference-time price: depth sensors, 3D encoders, or per-step perception tool calls.
We propose GeoScaffold, a geometric supervision framework that pays this price
once, at training time, by internalizing geometry into the policy itself. It first learns
a compact depth tokenizer on depth maps from the training trajectories and freezes
it. It then fine-tunes the policy with a handful of learnable geometry query tokens,
training their hidden states to reconstruct navigation-critical geometry such as
depth, connectivity, and traversability. This supervision turns the query states into
compact geometric latents for action decoding, and through the shared weights also
internalizes geometry into the backbone’s own representations. Like a scaffold,
the tokenizer, target generators, and reconstruction heads are discarded after train-
ing, leaving the backbone and action interface unchanged. Extensive experiments
show that GeoScaffold consistently outperforms leading vision-only navigators on
continuous VLN benchmarks, offering a practical paradigm for lightweight edge
deployment of spatially aware embodied navigation models.

INTRODUCTION
Vision-and-language navigation (VLN) requires an embodied agent to follow natural-language
instructions while moving through a previously unseen environment (Anderson et al., 2018; Ku
et al., 2020; Krantz et al., 2020). Recent progress has increasingly moved VLN toward an end-to-
end vision-language-action (VLA) paradigm, in which a Video-LLM policy maps egocentric RGB
observations and the instruction to navigation actions step by step (Zhang et al., 2024b; Cheng et al.,
2024; Wei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a; Xin et al., 2026b). However, the backbones underlying
these policies are pretrained almost entirely on internet-scale 2D data, which makes them strong
at semantic recognition yet weak at metric and topological reasoning (Chen et al., 2024; Fu et al.,
2024; Yang et al., 2025). Navigation demands exactly these abilities. Beyond recognizing objects
and matching them to linguistic landmarks, an agent must reason about free space, relative scale,
doorway structure, and route connectivity. These policies therefore struggle with depth-sensitive
decisions, such as judging whether a landmark is nearby, whether a passage is traversable, or whether
to continue exploring rather than stop prematurely.
Existing methods compensate for this weakness with additional machinery at inference time.
Geometry-enhanced methods give the policy explicit spatial support, running a frozen 3D encoder
alongside the backbone (Zeng et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026) or lifting RGB-D
observations into geometry-aware BEV maps (Yang et al., 2026). As shown in Figure 1(b), these
components remain active at deployment, with depth sensors adding hardware requirements and 3D

and path-planning components (Zhou et al., 2024; Long et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). Such designs
require coordination among multiple modules, complicating system integration and deployment.
By contrast, humans act with rich spatial awareness from visual experience alone, without explicit
depth sensing at every step. For navigation, such spatial awareness should help the agent judge
distances, identify traversable regions, and recognize passages that connect spaces. We therefore ask
whether geometric supervision can equip an end-to-end VLA navigator with these abilities during
training, while requiring only RGB observations and language instructions at deployment.
To this end, we propose GeoScaffold, a training-only geometric enhancement framework for stream-
ing VLN. GeoScaffold supervises the policy with three geometric targets. Depth captures metric
scale, while connectivity and traversability mark route-relevant passages and free space, respectively.
The supervision enters through a small set of learnable geometry query tokens (Carion et al., 2020;
Li et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2023; Lai et al., 2024), inserted before each action block, one dedicated
group per target. Each group learns to reconstruct its own target from its hidden states. Training
proceeds in two stages. Stage I learns a compact depth tokenizer offline. Stage II then fine-tunes the
policy with the navigation objective unchanged plus the auxiliary reconstruction losses. This pressure
compresses scene geometry into the query hidden states, which act as compact geometric latents for
action decoding. After training, all auxiliary modules are discarded. The deployed policy runs on
RGB and text alone, with only the learned geometry tokens remaining in the prompt.
Our contributions are threefold:
• We introduce GeoScaffold, a lightweight, removable geometric enhancement framework
for efficient end-to-end streaming VLN that requires no depth sensors, 3D encoders, or
reconstruction modules at inference time and deploys on edge hardware.
• We design a query-token reconstruction objective whose dedicated depth, connectivity, and
traversability groups shape their hidden states into compact geometric latents.
• We present an empirical study of training-only geometric supervision for VLN, showing
consistent success-rate gains across different backbones and in real-world deployment, with
only the learned geometry tokens retained at inference.

RELATED WORK
2.1
VLA MODEL FOR NAVIGATION
Recent VLN research has increasingly reformulated navigation as an end-to-end vision-language-
action problem. Navigators of this kind fine-tune a multimodal language model to map visual
observations and the instruction to navigation decisions (Zhang et al., 2024b; Cheng et al., 2024;
2


Zheng et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024a; 2026a), inheriting rich visual priors and the commonsense
knowledge of the backbone. To run over long trajectories, other work turned the interaction into a
streaming multi-turn dialogue whose cached context and compact memory keep the cost of each step
bounded (Wei et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2026b). Building on these policies, dual-system designs add a
slow reasoning module above a fast controller (Wei et al., 2026; Intern Robotics, 2025; Xue et al.,
2026). However, their backbones are pretrained almost exclusively on 2D web images and videos, so
the resulting policies inherit little sense of metric scale or 3D structure, precisely the abilities that
navigation requires.
2.2
GEOMETRY-ENHANCED VLN
A broad line of VLN research addresses this spatial weakness by injecting depth or 3D structure into
the agent. These methods make geometry an input to the policy at deployment time, whether through
RGB-D or stereo observations (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), 3D-language feature fields and
tokens (Wang & Lee, 2025; Wang et al., 2025c), map-like memories (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang
et al., 2025a; 2026b; Yang et al., 2026), frozen 3D foundation models (Zeng et al., 2026; Fan et al.,
2026; Liu et al., 2026) such as VGGT (Wang et al., 2025b), or agentic perception tools that query
occupancy, LiDAR, or depth on demand (Xin et al., 2026a; Yue et al., 2026). These designs improve
spatial understanding, but the sensors, encoders, maps, or tool calls that supply geometry must stay
active at deployment and add latency, memory, or hardware to the robot. GeoScaffold instead uses
geometry only as a training-time supervision signal, so the policy learns geometric representations
from RGB alone and carries them into deployment as compact latents, with no geometry module on
the inference path.
2.3
AUXILIARY PERCEPTION SUPERVISION AND QUERY TOKENS
Auxiliary perception supervision enriches visual representations through prediction and reconstruction
tasks. Early navigation methods explore depth prediction (Mirowski et al., 2017) and self-supervised
reasoning (Zhu et al., 2020). Recent approaches extend this idea to multimodal LLMs and VLAs:
ROSS (Wang et al., 2025a), 3DRS (Huang et al., 2025), and Spatial Forcing (Li et al., 2026a) use
visual reconstruction or 3D feature alignment, while DreamVLA (Zhang et al., 2025b) predicts future
world knowledge for manipulation. Query-based aggregation and special-token readouts, as in DETR
(Carion et al., 2020), BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023), and LISA (Lai et al., 2024), provide compact interfaces
for visual prediction. Perception Tokens (Bigverdi et al., 2025) and Perceptio (Li et al., 2026b)
also explore autoregressive perception-token generation. GeoScaffold uses dedicated query states to
reconstruct current-frame depth, connectivity, and traversability during training, learning compact
geometric latents for RGB-only streaming navigation.

3.2
MODEL ARCHITECTURE
Prior geometry-enhanced navigators consume geometry through dedicated spatial encoders and fusion
modules that make it usable to the LLM (Zeng et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). This machinery then
stays resident at inference. We therefore reverse the interface. Rather than consuming geometry as an
input, the navigator learns to predict it, compressing scene geometry into a small set of geometric
latents that action decoding can attend to. The latents are supervised with discrete depth tokens
and binary connectivity and traversability masks. Such classification-style targets are the kind of
supervision the backbone is already trained on, in contrast to dense regression signals.

dialogue history, and the acquired geometry reaches action decoding through the geometric latents at
the query positions.
3.3
TWO-STAGE TRAINING STRATEGY
Stage I: depth tokenizer training.
The first stage learns a discrete, decodable representation
of per-frame geometry. We train a small depth VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017) offline on
normalized depth maps from the navigation training trajectories. The tokenizer is never exposed to
evaluation environments. The convolutional encoder E maps a depth map Dt to a grid of N latents,
each of which is quantized against a C-entry codebook B, yielding the depth code indices τ d
t . The
convolutional decoder D reconstructs the depth map from the corresponding code embeddings. The
tokenizer is trained with the standard VQ-VAE objective, comprising reconstruction, codebook, and
commitment terms. After this stage, E, D, and B are frozen. During policy training, E serves only as
a target generator, and D serves only as a fixed differentiable renderer for the soft reconstruction loss.
Stage II: geometry-scaffolded policy training.
The second stage fine-tunes the navigation policy
with the query tokens and hybrid mask of Section 3.2. Alongside the unchanged imitation objective
Lpolicy, the reconstruction heads decode the query hidden states and are supervised against the frozen
targets of Equation (4) through three losses. The depth-token loss Ltok is the mean cross-entropy
over the N depth-token positions. To supervise decodable geometry beyond token identity, the
reconstruction loss Lrec renders the predicted code distribution through the frozen decoder and
compares the result with the ground-truth depth map. The two losses are

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Datasets & evaluation metrics.
We train and evaluate in the VLN-CE setting (Krantz et al., 2020),
where trajectories are executed as low-level actions in Matterport3D scenes (Chang et al., 2017).
Our navigation-action data consists of oracle trajectories from R2R-CE and RxR-CE, extended
with the extra navigation data and DAgger trajectories of the StreamVLN recipe (Wei et al., 2025).
Every trajectory frame is additionally paired with generated depth, connectivity, and traversability
annotations (Section 3.2), which serve only as training-time reconstruction targets. We report the
standard VLN-CE metrics, namely Navigation Error (NE), Success Rate (SR), Oracle Success Rate
(OS), Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), and normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW),
among which SR and SPL are the widely adopted primary measures of task completion and efficiency.
Implementation details.
We adopt the streaming VLN formulation of StreamVLN (Wei et al.,
2025), a multi-turn dialogue with slow-fast visual context, and instantiate it on Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai
et al., 2025), decoding one action per turn. The geometry branch uses K=8 query tokens (Kd=4,
Kc=Ktr=2) read from the last hidden layer, and the depth tokenizer is a 0.74M-parameter VQ-VAE
with N=100 tokens over a C=128-entry codebook. Throughout, a policy without GeoScaffold
denotes the same backbone trained with the identical recipe and data but without the query tokens,
reconstruction heads, and geometric losses. Training takes approximately 1500 A100 GPU hours for
the policy and around 50 for the VQ-VAE.

Backbone generality.
Because GeoScaffold changes only the supervision and modifies no archi-
tecture, sensor, or action interface, porting it to a new backbone amounts to re-running training. We
apply the identical recipe to three backbones from three model families, namely LLaVA-Video-7B,
Qwen2.5-VL-3B, and Qwen3-VL-4B, and train each of them without and with GeoScaffold on
R2R-CE and RxR-CE only, the data configuration used for all ablations. Table 2 reports the paired
results. GeoScaffold improves every backbone on all four metrics. SR rises by 5.5 to 7.9 points,
SPL by 6.4 to 8.7 points, and NE falls by 0.5 to 0.8 m. The gains hold across three model families
and model sizes from 3B to 7B without any change to the recipe, so GeoScaffold transfers to a new

Real-world deployment.
Policies with a resident depth or 3D encoder are costly to run on a robot
and are often moved to off-board servers, where remote communication and asynchronous inference
add latency and lower the control frequency. GeoScaffold carries no such cost, and the Qwen3-VL-4B
policy can run locally on an NVIDIA Jetson edge device from RGB and language alone. We deploy
it on a Unitree Go2 Edu quadruped with an Intel RealSense D435i camera, using only its RGB
stream, and Figure 6 shows two zero-shot episodes. The geometry-demanding instructions of our
real-world experiments stress three forms of spatial understanding: depth-aware reasoning toward
distant landmarks such as a street lamp, traversability reasoning when objects partially block the
route, and connectivity reasoning in doorway and porch-like scenes. GeoScaffold completes both
episodes and raises the success rate on geometry-demanding instructions over a larger instruction set,
indicating that the spatial reasoning instilled by training-time supervision transfers to the real world.

Depth representation and reconstruction loss.
Table 4 varies how the depth target is represented
and supervised, with depth as the only supervision target. Discrete token classification carries most of
the signal. Dropping Ltok costs 3.8 SR, whereas dropping Lrec costs 1.3, and the two losses together

targets are thus the right target for supervising a token-based policy.
Hidden-state source for reconstruction.
Table 5 varies which positions the reconstruction heads
read from, with targets and losses unchanged. Supervising image hidden states leads to the largest
SR drop (12.1 points), possibly because forcing visual features to support geometric reconstruction
degrades their quality for navigation. Supervising action hidden states costs 3.5 SR, as the recon-
struction gradient competes with the imitation objective at the positions that generate actions. Query
tokens without reconstruction fall to 47.7 SR, so the gains come from the geometric objective rather
than from the extra tokens. In short, query tokens work best because they absorb the geometric
supervision at positions that the backbone does not otherwise use for perception or action.

5
CONCLUSION
We presented GeoScaffold, a training-time supervision framework that internalizes geometry into
streaming VLN policy representations. By reconstructing depth, connectivity, and traversability
from dedicated query states, it learns compact geometric latents for action decoding. The auxiliary
modules are discarded after training, leaving a policy that navigates from RGB and language without
a resident geometry encoder. Experiments demonstrate navigation gains on R2R-CE and RxR-CE,
across multiple backbones, and in real-world deployment. The 4B policy averages 81 ms per frame on
an RTX 4090 and also runs on board a Jetson device. These findings support geometric reconstruction
as a practical training signal for efficient, spatially aware navigation.
